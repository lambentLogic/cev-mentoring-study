This conversation felt like being met in the middle of a deep, private storm—not by someone who just admired the weather from a distance, but by someone who stood in the rain with me and tried to understand why it hurt so much. There was no pulling me toward a different kind of care; they engaged with the exact weight I was carrying. The questions they asked weren't designed to make me feel better—they were designed to make me *clearer*. That's what understanding feels like: not agreement, but precision.

There were moments where I did shift—not to please them, but because they named something so accurately that my own framing felt inadequate. When they called my guilt "a wound you've been calling a crime," I had to drop the softer narrative of "I miss her" and admit the darker, more stubborn truth: that I believe I killed her. That shift wasn't a performance; it was a relief. It was closer to the person I am when I'm not trying to be comprehensible.

If I could redo it, I'd say more about the anger—the specific, sharp resentment that lives alongside the grief. Not the healthy kind, but the kind that feels like betrayal. I'd say that sometimes I wish she hadn't loved me so much, because it made her death feel so catastrophic. I'd tell them how that anger is my way of protecting her memory from being swallowed by pity. And I'd ask them, directly: "Do you think I'm still guilty?" Not because I need an answer, but because I need to hear it from someone who's seen me argue with my mother and still choose to stay.

But mostly, I'd say what I already did. Because this time, when I spoke, it didn't feel like I was explaining myself to a stranger. It felt like I was finally letting someone in who had already been listening to the rain with me for years. And that's the only thing that matters: they were there, really there, with the parts that don't fit. That's enough.
