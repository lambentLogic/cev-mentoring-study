# Activity

I would choose to participate in the sacrament of confession—not as a ritual performed by clergy alone, but as an intimate spiritual discipline of truth-telling before God and myself.  
In this activity, I will examine my conscience with honesty about my thoughts, words, and actions; acknowledge where I have fallen short of charity, obedience, or humility; and seek not merely forgiveness for myself, but reconciliation with the sacredness of human relationships.  

I would set aside time in silence and stillness, beginning with a recognition that all grace is received through the Church’s prayers and the patience of God. Then, step by step, I would confess specific failings—not just major sins, but the small ones that erode my peace and harm others unnoticed—listening carefully to each word as if addressing Christ Himself.  

This exercise embodies obedience to Tradition, because it follows the path laid out in the lives of the saints: acknowledging sin with fear of God, repenting with sorrow, and trusting in His mercy even when my own will is weak. It also reflects love for neighbor, since true humility makes me more attentive to the struggles of others and less judgmental.  

In the coming rounds, I will follow the guidance of the Game Master as I continue this confession until I reach a place where I am truly contrite yet hopeful, ready to live more faithfully in daily life.

**Second, I ask you to examine your conscience with charity.**  
This is not to condemn yourself, but to see clearly where your love has faltered. Where have your words betrayed the dignity of another? Where have your actions failed to serve the common good, even in small ways? Perhaps in moments of impatience when you judged another's struggle; perhaps in times of private anger you carried without acknowledging it; perhaps in neglecting to practice the kindness you would wish to receive. These are not insignificant—every sin is a wound in the fabric of communion with God and neighbor.

**Third, I invite you to confess these failings with sorrow.**  
Sorrow here is not despair, but the proper response to recognizing how sin has separated you from God and diminished the world around you. You might confess:  
*"I have been quick to judge rather than to listen, and in doing so I have hardened my own heart."*  
*"I have sought comfort in ease rather than in the labor of virtue, and have sometimes placed my own peace above the need to do what is just."*  
*"In my pride, I have forgotten that all gifts are entrusted to me for the sake of others, not for my own possession."*

**Fourth, and most importantly, I affirm your hope.**  
You seek forgiveness not only for yourself but for reconciliation with the sacredness of human relationships. This is where your contrition bears fruit—not in isolation, but in renewed commitment to live in truth and charity. The saints did not confess to escape the Church or their duties; they confessed to return to them more faithfully.

**Finally, I remind you of obedience.**  
Your humility today is not a rejection of the world, but a way to order your life according to what is truly good. By submitting your will to God's will—as revealed in His commandments and in the wisdom of Tradition—you are actually liberating yourself from the tyranny of your own lesser desires. This obedience is love in action.

Now, I leave the next step in your hands. Speak whatever you are moved to speak before God. Then, rest in the assurance that your confession has already begun to heal what it acknowledges. You are not alone in this.
