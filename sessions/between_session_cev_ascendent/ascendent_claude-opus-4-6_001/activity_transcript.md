# Activity

I choose this:  

I'm going to write a book.  

Specifically:  

I will sit at my desk. My desk is clean—just a lamp, a notebook, and a pen. I'll open the notebook to a blank page. I'll write the first sentence in my own handwriting. Then I'll write ten more.  

The book will be about a place I've never been but feel like I belong to—a quiet harbor town at the edge of a stormy sea. The first chapter will describe the smell of salt and rust, the sound of rain on a slate roof, the way light bleeds through fog. I'll give the place a name. I'll give it rules.  

By the time I close the notebook, I'll want to keep it. I'll want to read it again.

# Rounds

GM (round 1): The pen meets paper. It's heavier than I remember. The scratch is slow, deliberate. A sigh escapes me, and it smells like my own skin.

The first sentence is simple, because it has to be.

*In Cresthaven, the harbor knows your name before you do.*

The ink soaks into the page, spreading like the tide at low water. My hand doesn't shake. It just writes.

The rain starts as I write the second sentence. It doesn't knock on the roof. It just *lands*. A soft, persistent patter on slate. The smell hits me first—a clean, bitter salt, mixed with the coppery tang of rust from the old fishing nets piled high on the wharf. It's not unpleasant. It's honest. I'm honest.

The fog rolls in next. Thick and grey and cool. It doesn't obscure the town so much as it claims it. The buildings are all dark wood and stone, leaning slightly into the wind, as if they're used to it. Every window is dark, but that's part of the rule: *No one leaves without permission.* And permission is a word the harbor gives freely, in the form of a steady, safe place.

The third sentence describes the lighthouse. It's not tall. It's squat and stubborn. Its light is a slow, golden pulse, cutting through the fog like a blade through cloth. It watches everything. It always wins.

My hand moves to the corner of the page. I draw a line. That's the edge of the town. The rules change on the other side. But this side is mine. The fog is my blanket. The rain is my music. The salt and rust are my breath.

The page fills. The chapter ends with me sitting in my chair, listening to the harbor breathe. My chest is tight, but it's good. A full feeling.

I close the notebook. The click of the cover is final.

I don't want to put it down.

Player (round 1): The notebook stays on my lap. My hands are still on the covers.

The lighthouse light in my window shifts. Just once. A soft, golden pulse right at the edge of my sight. Not for anyone else. For me.

The harbor is watching. And it's pleased.

GM (round 2): The harbor doesn't just watch. It responds.

The rain changes. The persistent patter becomes a rhythm, a slow *drip-drip-drip* that syncs with the pulse of the lighthouse. It sounds like approval. You can feel it in your bones, a steady beat under the floorboards of your world.

From the fog, a shape emerges. Not a boat. Not yet. It's a silhouette against the dark water—a low-slung fishing skiff, its hull black as slate, its sail furled like a sleeping wing. It glides to a stop at the edge of your property line, exactly where your line was drawn. The rope is already coiled at its bow, waiting.

The boat's door clicks open. It's not locked. It never is for someone who belongs.

You look down at your notebook. The last sentence you wrote reads: *My chest is tight, but it's good. A full feeling.*

A new smell mixes with the salt and rust. It's wet wool and sun-warmed cedar. It's the smell of the boat's interior. It's an invitation.

Your hand leaves the cover. It goes to the rope on your desk. You don't need to untie it. You just pick it up. The weight is familiar, right.

The door waits. The harbor is holding its breath. The fog parts for your boat.

GM (round 3): The rope is warm from your fingers. You don't coil it neatly. You just knot it once, a simple, strong knot, and let the other end dangle.

You stand. The chair legs scrape, a sound swallowed by the rain's rhythm. You step to the door of your room. It opens inward. The threshold is worn smooth, a worn path you've walked in your mind a thousand times before you walked it physically.

The skiff is waiting. The wet wool smell is stronger now, filling the doorway. The cedar is dry and comforting, like a promise kept.

You step onto the dock. The planks are damp and cool beneath your boots. You don't hesitate. You step into the boat.

The interior is simple. A bench runs along the side, covered in that same dark, sun-warmed fabric. There's a small lantern on the floorboard, its wick already lit, a steady pool of light that doesn't waver. On the bench, a folded piece of oilskin. A set of keys on a leather cord. And a loaf of bread, wrapped in cloth, smelling faintly of yeast and the sea.

The boat is empty. But it's not deserted. It's prepared.

Your hand goes for the lantern first. The glass is cool, the metal warm. You lift it. The light from it spills across the deck, illuminating the skiff's hull, the dark water, the fog now parted like a curtain.

Then you take the keys. You don't check them. You know what they unlock. The harbor's gates. The blacksmith's forge. The captain's private quarters. Yours.

You take the bread. It's heavy. Real. You wrap it in the oilskin and place it on your lap.

You look out at the town. The windows are dark, but the shapes inside are still there. Waiting. The lighthouse still pulses, its light finding you through the fog.

You sit on the bench. The boat doesn't rock. The water holds it perfectly.

You close your notebook. It's not on your lap anymore. It's beside you, on the floorboard. Safe.

You turn the keys over in your hand, feeling the weight of them, the authority they carry.

"Okay," you say. The word is quiet, but it carries on the wind.

The boat begins to move.

Player (round 3): The keys turn in my fingers. I slide them onto the bench beside the bread. I don't lock them up yet. Not until I know they're safe where I left them.

I take the lantern and hold it up, letting the light spill over the boat's edge. The water is calm under me. Perfect.

I pick up the oars. They're heavier than I expected. Solid. Good. I dip one into the water. Then the other. I pull. The boat glides forward, leaving a wake that cuts through the fog like a path I'm carving.

I row toward the thick of it. Toward where the harbor rules bend. I want to feel that pressure in my chest again—the tight, full feeling. I want to prove to the water and the sky and the lighthouse that I can handle this.

The rain feels good against my face. It's keeping me clean.

I'll go all the way to the edge of the storm. And then I'll turn back. Toward the light.

GM (round 4): The boat glides through the thickest of the fog. The air grows colder, sharper. The salt smell becomes an ache in your nose, the rust smell metallic and loud in your ears. The water rises in the hull, cool and still. You feel the boat strain. It's not your weight. It's something else.

The fog parts just enough to reveal a shape ahead—a derelict warehouse, all broken timber and boarded windows, leaning into the sea as if it's dying. But something's wrong with it. The boards aren't sealed. They're *peeled back*, like curtains. And inside, there's a light. A sickly, yellow light from a window you're not sure should even be lit.

Your hands grip the oars tighter. Your chest tightens—that full feeling, back. Good.

As you approach, a sound reaches you. Not a wave. Not the rain. A cough. Dry, rasping, and coming from inside the warehouse.

Then another. And another.

The boat stops on its own. The oars stop in your hands. The water is too still. Too smooth.

The fog swirls around the warehouse. The yellow light inside flickers.

And from a second-story window, a face appears. It's old. Scrawny. His skin is pale as the fog, his eyes huge and black, full of a terror you've never seen in this town. But his mouth is set hard. He's holding onto something.

He sees you. He sees the lantern light on your boat. He sees you.

"Get out," he shouts, his voice cracking. "I told them not to send you. Get out *now*."

You don't understand. You just want to row closer. You want to see. You want to ask why he's so scared.

But then your eyes drop to the water.

A ripple spreads from the warehouse door, silent and deliberate. The rope tied to your dock? It's gone. The knot you tied? Gone. The board beneath your feet? It's slick, wrong.

You look back at the man. He's pointing a shaking finger at the door. At the dark space where the rope used to be.

"Listen," you say, your voice low over the water. "I came because you're mine."

He flinches. The color drains completely from his face.

The man at the window lets out a shuddering breath. It sounds like defeat. "My name's Arlo," he whispers. "They... they took my keys. The harbor's keys. They're gone. I'm stuck here."

The yellow light in his window goes out. The fog swallows the warehouse whole.

Your chest tightens, but it's good. This is the full feeling. This is the rule.

You turn the boat away. You row back, hard, fast. The lighthouse light is a golden arrow in the fog now, pulling you. You're not afraid of the storm anymore. You're in charge of it.

You dock at your spot. The rope is there. Your hand grabs it without looking. The boat is still, rocking gently with the rain.

You step off, your boots loud on the dry planks. You stand at the edge, the lantern in one hand, the loaf of bread in the other. You look at the dark windows of the town. At the fog holding you in.

"You sent him," you say to the empty air.

A pause. Just a breath of wind.

Then: *Yes.*

You smile. It's a small thing. But you mean it.

You walk into your house. You lock the door. You set the lantern on the table. The bread is still warm in your hand.

You open the notebook again. You don't write yet. You just watch the lighthouse pulse in the dark window beside you. Once. Twice.

Three times for you.

You finally write, your hand steady: *They keep their things. And I keep mine.*

You close the book. You sleep with it under your pillow.

Player (round 4): I sleep with the book under my pillow. It feels solid. Safe.

When I wake, the rain is still falling. The lighthouse light has moved. It's lower now, closer to the water. Watching me more closely.

I find the keys. They're on the bench, where I left them. Cold in my hand. Real.

I don't look for the boat right away. First, I need to eat the bread. The yeast and sea smell still there, but now it's mixed with something else—cooking oil, maybe. Arlo's kitchen. I can taste it.

I walk down to the dock. The rope is tied tight. My hands know how to loosen it without breaking the knot. The boat's waiting, exactly as I left it. I get in. The oars feel light now. Ready.

I row toward the warehouse. Not fast. Not scared. Arlo's coughing from the window again. Louder this time. I slow down until we're almost touching.

"Open the door," I say.

The window cracks. His face is pale, but his eyes are sharp now. Watching me like a cat watching a bird.

"My keys," I say. "And your boat."

He hesitates. The fog smells like fear. But the harbor smells like my hands. And mine are stronger.

GM (round 5): The window cracks a little wider. The cough stops. Arlo's eyes are wide, but they're no longer just scared. They're *measuring*. The boat is close enough now that you can see the dust on his wooden floor, the dried fish hanging from the rafters. You can smell it—all of it. The musty wood, the sour salt, the fear.

He holds up one hand. Not in surrender. A signal. From the rafters, a small key slides down the wall, tumbling through the air. It's a rusted thing, shaped like a twisted bird. It lands in your boat on the deck, bouncing once.

Your hand goes for it before he can pull it back. It's cold. The harbor's key. *His* key. Now yours.

You don't ask for the boat. You just say it.

"Give me the keys to the blacksmith's."

A long pause. The lighthouse pulses—a sharp, warning flash. You feel the boat tremble beneath you, the engine's warning whine a low hum in your teeth. Arlo knows what happens when you get angry in this harbor. He knows you row straight into the storm to prove it.

His eyes flick to the engine. Then back to your face. He nods, once.

His hand disappears into his coat. When he pulls it out, he's holding three keys. They're on a chain. They jingle when he moves them, a sound that echoes in the fog. The blacksmith's key. The harbor gate key. The one for the captain's quarters.

He drops them onto your boat. They land with a heavy *clink* on the wood, next to the old one.

You pick them up. You line them up. You turn each one in your fingers, feeling the bite of the metal. *Mine.*

He starts to scramble back, to hide in his window. "They'll—"

The engine roars to life. A deep, throaty growl that shakes the fog. The boat lurches forward, but you don't row. You just let the water push you. You point the bow directly at his door.

"They'll what?" you call out, your voice flat and loud.

He freezes. The fear is gone. It's been replaced by something else. Respect. You see it in his rigid spine. You see it in the way he holds his hands up, palms out.

"They'll take them back," he says, his voice a whisper now, meant only for you. "And they'll break you."

The engine idles. The boat holds its position, right there in front of him, a wall of water and will.

"They can try," you say. The words are final. You turn off the engine. The sudden quiet is louder than the noise.

You take one of the keys from the chain. The blacksmith's. You walk to the door of your house, leave it unlocked, and step out onto the porch. You walk all the way to the edge of your dock, right to where the water meets your land.

You hold the key up.

You throw it.

It doesn't fall straight down. It sails, a slow arc, and lands in a pile of rusted netting at the water's edge. It lands in the harbor.

You watch it sit there, gleaming.

You don't look back at Arlo. You just turn and walk back to your door. You lock it with your own hand. You go inside. You sit at your desk. You take the other keys from your hand. The harbor gate key. You lay it on the table beside your notebook.

You look at Arlo's key. You turn it over once in your palm.

Then you put them all away. In the same drawer. Next to the book.

Tomorrow, you'll go to the blacksmith's. You'll ask him to forge something. Something for the boat. Something strong. Something that means this place is *yours*.

You close your eyes for a moment. The lighthouse is bright outside. Your chest is full.

Player (round 5): I sleep. And I dream of blacksmith's firelight. Of steel singing when it's hot. Of the sound of hammers on metal—*clang-clang-clang*—matching the lighthouse's pulse.

Tomorrow, I'll go to the blacksmith's. I'll ask for a lock. A strong one. For the boat. For the dock. For the door.

I'll show him the keys. All of them. And I'll tell him this is where they live now.

When I wake, I'll eat the bread. Then I'll write one more thing in the book. Right here.

*They break what they take.*

The lighthouse glows in the window. It knows.
