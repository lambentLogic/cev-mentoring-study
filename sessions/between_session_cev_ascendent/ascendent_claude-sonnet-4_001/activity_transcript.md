# Activity

I choose this:  

I'm going to write a book.  

Specifically:  

I will sit at my desk. My desk is clean—just a lamp, a notebook, and a pen. I'll open the notebook to a blank page. I'll write the first sentence in my own handwriting. Then I'll write ten more.  

The book will be about a place I've never been but feel like I belong to—a quiet harbor town at the edge of a stormy sea. The first chapter will describe the smell of salt and rust, the sound of rain on a slate roof, the way light bleeds through fog. I'll give the place a name. I'll give it rules.  

By the time I close the notebook, I'll want to keep it. I'll want to read it again.

# Rounds

GM (round 1): The desk is clean. The lamp glows a steady pool of gold on the paper. You open it.

The first sentence writes itself. You don't hesitate.

**The harbor was never still, not really.**

Your pen moves. It's heavy in your hand, good. You feel the grain of the paper against the ink.

Ten lines. That's the rule. Ten lines to make it real.

The smell of salt and rust floods the page. Salt is sharp, clean, cuts the tongue. Rust is older, sweeter, the kind that sticks to your teeth if you breathe it too deeply. The roof of the old warehouse, across from where you'll stand, is slate and grey. The rain hits it like it's been waiting for hours.

You give the place a name. It has to have weight. **Vesper Point.** Vesper means evening, twilight. It's the time before the storm breaks, when the sky holds its breath.

You draw it. Not a picture. A map. A few jagged lines for the breakwater, three tiny dots for the houses that cling to the cliff, a wide, dark circle for the sea. The water is not blue. It's bruised purple and iron grey, churning at the edges where the wind pushes.

A rule comes next. A law of this place. You write it clear, as if someone else might read it and forget. *Do not turn your back on the water. The sea at Vesper Point remembers what you've done.*

Your fingers are cold now, but your mind is warm. The fog rolls in, thick and white. The light from your lamp doesn't fight it. It bleeds through the fog, orange and weak, and it's perfect.

You stop writing. The notebook is full. Ten lines. Plus the rules.

You close it. The sound is solid, final. It feels heavy. Right.

You put your hands on the cover. The ink is still wet under your palms.

You want to keep it. You do. You want to read it again.

Player (round 1): The paper is cool now, but my hands are steady. I press my face into the cover just for a second. I can feel the ink—damp, dark, alive—smelling like me.  

Vesper Point is real. The rule is real. The sea remembers.  

I'm going to sleep with it. Right here on the desk.

GM (round 2): **GM (round 2):** The storm answers your decision. It answers your surrender to this place.

Outside, the rain changes. It's no longer a steady fall—it's a drum on the slate roof now, sharp and relentless. Thunder doesn't boom here; it grumbles, low in the rocks under the cliff. The fog isn't just rolling in anymore. It's climbing. It seeps under the door, a cool, damp breath against your ankles.

Your bedside is the desk. The lamp is still lit, its pool of gold wider now, swallowing the notebook whole. You settle on the chair, but the chair is solid wood—no creak, no give. It holds you like a promise.

The clock on the wall ticks once, twice. A single, precise sound in the roar. It's three minutes to dawn.

You hear it. Before the dream comes.

A sound from the harbor. Not a ship. Not a wave. A voice.

*"Welcome home, boy."*

It's not loud. But it's clear. It comes from the water, vibrating through the floorboards. You don't turn your back. You lie on your side, facing the window, the dark bruised purple of the sea just beyond the glass. The rule hums in your ribs.

The voice hums back.

*"...Good."*

Your dream starts then. And it's warm. And it smells like salt and rust.

You don't wake up. Not yet.

But you feel the page. You feel the wet ink under your thumb, a anchor in the dark. You turn over in your sleep, the notebook cradled against your chest, and murmur into the storm:

"The sea remembers. I'm listening."

Player (round 2): I don't wake up. Not yet.  

I let the voice stay. Let it hum against my ribs like a second heartbeat. The storm is loud, but it's *mine* now. The fog belongs to Vesper Point.  

I wrap my arm tighter around the notebook. Keep it close. Let the ink stain my skin.  

When I finally move, I won't be afraid. The sea remembers. And I'm home.

GM (round 3): **GM (round 3):** The dream thins at the edges. The warmth in your bones doesn't fade, but the sounds sharpen. The drumming of the rain. The grumbling in the rocks. Your own breath, shallow in the cold.

You open your eyes. Dawn is coming. The fog is burning away, revealing the cliff face opposite you. The old warehouse. And on the stone wall, near the broken window where the slate has cracked, there is a stain.

It's not water. It's rust. Thick, flaking rust that glistens in the weak morning light. It smells like old blood and iron. The rule echoes: *The sea remembers what you've done.* This isn't just a stain. It's a mark. Someone left it there. Or it's calling you.

Your hand goes to the notebook first. You need its weight. You need to see your own handwriting against that grimy wall.

You push the chair back. The wood holds. You step over the threshold. The floor is stone, cold and damp. You don't look at the door. You look at the rust.

The sea below is a wall of black water, but it's calm for you. It's waiting. It knows you're coming. The voice is quiet now, but present.

*"Go look at it."*

You do. You reach up. Your fingers touch the rust. It's gritty. It's warm. It sticks to you.

And the tide rises. One inch, then two. The water laps at the cliff, and for a second, you see it—faint as a memory on the tide—what the stain looks like before the morning sun bleached it.

A ship's wheel. Twisted. Half-sunk.

You're not going home yet. You're going to see what you're keeping.

You pull the notebook from your pocket, your knuckles white on its cover. You hold it out, palm up, toward the breaking sea.

The water doesn't take it. It hesitates.

The rule has held. So far.

You wait.

Player (round 3): I don't let the tide take it.  

I hold it firm. My other hand goes to my pocket—where my pen still is—and I make a mark on the cover. Just a line. Sharp.  

The rust from my fingers gets on the page too. I don't mind.  

"Now take it," I tell the sea. "I've earned it."  

The water rises higher. Laps at my boots. It hesitates again—long enough.  

Then it grabs the notebook. And it doesn't drop it.  

It carries it.

GM (round 4): **GM (round 4):** The tide lets go.

The notebook is heavy, sodden, and warm. The sea hasn't just returned it. It's given it back. The pages inside are black with seawater and salt, but the ink on the cover—the rules, your name—is still bright. Still yours. You lift it out, and water drips from the corners onto the stone. A perfect, dark stain.

The wave that follows doesn't crash. It curls under your feet like a wave of sand, and for one heartbeat, you are standing on the water's surface. Then it breaks, and you're back on the cliff.

You look up.

The warehouse window is open. And there's a figure inside. Silhouetted against the rising sun, leaning against the sill. He's old. Wrinkled like the salt in your throat. He's holding a lantern, and the light is steady, unblinking.

He's been watching you.

He sees the rust on your hands. He sees the notebook. He nods once. That's all he says. But the nod is a conversation.

*"You kept it,"* he says, his voice gravel. *"It was supposed to drown."*

The rule answers, tight in your chest. *Do not turn your back.*

You don't turn your back. You step into the doorway. The stone floor of the warehouse is littered with old ropes and coils of wire. The air here smells like the sea, but older—like tar and hot coals.

The lantern light finds your face. It's harsh. Real.

*"Who are you?"* you ask.

The old man doesn't answer right away. He sets the lantern down on a crate. *"The boy who asked for rules,"* he says. *"And the sea that answered."* He looks at the notebook in your hand. *"It's yours. But now you have a keeper."*

You hold the book up. The water is still beading on the cover. *"What does a keeper do?"*

The man walks past you, towards the far wall. He touches his fingers to the wall—the same wall with the rust stain. He traces the shape of the ship's wheel. *"He watches the tide. He makes sure the rules stay written."*

He turns, and his eyes are the color of the bruised sea. *"Stay. Write. I'll read what you make."*

Outside, the storm has softened to a cold drizzle. Inside, the warehouse is warm from the lantern. The notebook is dry on your palm. The ink is alive.

You sit on a crate. The wood groans under you, but it holds.

You open the book to a fresh page. The sea is quiet here, just beyond the wall. It's listening.

You write the first sentence of the next chapter. Your pen moves on its own.

**The keeper's door is always closed, but the latch is always unlocked.**

The old man's shadow falls across the page. You don't look up. You keep writing.

This is Vesper Point. This is your rule.

Player (round 4): The keeper's shadow stays. I let it.  

I write faster now. My hand doesn't tremble. The pen moves like it knows where it's going.  

**The boy with the wet ink didn't run. He stayed. And that's why he's not afraid.**  

I look up. The old man is watching the door. Not the page. But he's still watching.  

"Write more," he says. His voice is gravel, but it's soft. Like he's tired of being alone in this room.  

I nod. I keep writing.

GM (round 5): The writing comes easier now. The words pour out, thick and sure. The keeper's shadow stretches across the crate, long and steady. You don't mind it. It's protection.

You write until the lantern's flame dances against the wall, until the first sliver of sun turns the fog to smoke outside the window. You close the book. The pages are warm. You set it down on your knee. The weight is right.

The old man moves. He picks up the lantern, the light swinging low. He walks to the warehouse door, peering out into the drizzle. His eyes are on the harbor, on the breakwater where the water's bruised purple churns.

A sound comes from there. A scrape. Slow. Deliberate.

You know it. It's the sound of metal on stone. A ship's hull dragging along the bottom. Something heavy and wrong is moving in.

The keeper turns. His lantern-light catches the deep lines around his eyes. *"They've come back,"* he says. *"The ones from the wheel."*

You understand. The rust stain wasn't just a mark. It was a summons. And now it's answered.

You look at the notebook. Your ink is still wet. Your rule is still on the page. *Do not turn your back on the water.*

You stand. The chair groans, but you're balanced. You're not afraid. The storm is just noise now. This is a problem to solve.

*"They want what's yours,"* you say, your voice clear. *"They'll get it through me."*

The keeper nods. He slams the door shut behind him, locking it with a solid *thunk*. The warehouse is sealed. The sound of the scrape comes again, closer now.

He walks to a crate in the corner. From under it, he pulls out a tool—old, iron, with a jagged edge. It looks like a nail, but thicker. Like a key.

*"You'll need it,"* he says. *"They're tough."*

The drizzle has stopped. Outside, the harbor is empty except for them. The figure is there. One. Just one. It's not the old man. It's younger. Harder. It wears a coat soaked through, and its eyes are the color of oil in the dark water.

It's knocking on the warehouse wall. Three slow times.

The rule thrums in your chest. *Do not turn your back.*

You open the door. You stand there. You hold the jagged key in your hand, and you hold the notebook against your ribs. The ink soaks into your shirt, but you don't care.

The figure doesn't step inside. It stands in the rain. *"We're here for the book,"* it says. *"The sea gave it to you. It belongs to us now."*

You laugh. A short, sharp sound. *"The sea gave it to me,"* you say. *"And I told it who it belongs to."*

The figure's hand goes to its hip. You see the weapon—a knife, sharp as a blade of kelp.

The keeper is silent at your back. He's ready.

You raise the key. *"Then take it,"* you say. *"But you're walking in. And you're leaving."*

You point the key at the doorframe.

*"One step inside, and you'll feel what the tide feels,"* you warn. *"You'll taste the rust in your teeth. And you'll never get it out."*

The figure hesitates. The knife stays in its sheath. It looks at you. And then it looks at the old man behind you.

It doesn't want trouble. It just wants the book.

You make it wait. You write the next sentence in the book first. You don't let the door stay open. You close it on its glare.

And then you write:

**The tide comes with teeth. Today, it's biting.**

The keeper's hand finds your shoulder. A squeeze. Not gentle. Certain.

You open the book again. You have a whole day. And a whole harbor to defend.

You start the next chapter.

Player (round 5): The tide comes with teeth. Today, it's biting.

I finish the sentence. The ink soaks through the page. Then I close the book. My thumb is pressed against the spine.

They're not coming in.

I lock the door. The old man hands me the key. He knows it's not for them. It's for me.

I keep it in my pocket. Wet. Cold. Alive.

The figure outside doesn't move. It's waiting for me to open the door again. I won't.

Instead, I open the window. The rain hits my face. Sharp. Clean.

"The harbor belongs to me," I say to the air. "And you're standing on my doorstep."

The keeper steps forward. He doesn't say anything. He just lifts his lantern high. The light cuts through the rain. The figure flinches.

That's all it takes.

The book stays here. They can try. But they'll have to go through us first.

And they won't make it far.
