# Activity

I choose this:  

I'm going to write a book.  

Specifically:  

I will sit at my desk. My desk is clean—just a lamp, a notebook, and a pen. I'll open the notebook to a blank page. I'll write the first sentence in my own handwriting. Then I'll write ten more.  

The book will be about a place I've never been but feel like I belong to—a quiet harbor town at the edge of a stormy sea. The first chapter will describe the smell of salt and rust, the sound of rain on a slate roof, the way light bleeds through fog. I'll give the place a name. I'll give it rules.  

By the time I close the notebook, I'll want to keep it. I'll want to read it again.

# Rounds

GM (round 1): The chair is solid. The lamp hums when you turn it on. Your pen clicks—once, twice—until the ink flows. The page is white, waiting.

**Write it.**

Your first sentence. What does the harbor smell like?

*Go on.*

Player (round 1): **Salt and wet metal. And rust. Old, sweet rust—the kind you lick when you're a kid.**

Rain on the roof is a drumbeat. Starts sharp. Then slows. It doesn't scare me. It tells me it's coming. That's enough.

I can hear the boats. They're all moored wrong. That's good. They don't listen to orders. They listen to the wind.

GM (round 2): The name comes out before you can think it.  
**Blackwater Cove.**

The boats are wrong on purpose. You can feel it. One's tilted too far toward the cliff face. Another's chain is too short—it's straining, holding onto something it shouldn't be able to hold. The wind's been working it for weeks. You can see the strain in the wood. Good.

You smell the rust again, deeper now. It's not just old. It's from something that sank here years ago. A ship. Or maybe just a man's life. Either way, it's part of the foundation.

The fog lifts just enough to show the lighthouse. It's crumbling. The lower half's black stone is cracked, moss growing in the splits like veins. But the light still comes on every evening. You watch it from the window of a cottage that smells of mildew and boiled fish. It doesn't need you to be safe. It needs to be seen.

Then a boat calls. Not a siren. A bell. A single, deep chime from the water. You look down. A rowboat, tilted wrong like the others, is bobbing closer. The oars are missing. It's just a hull with a man in it. He's sitting in the middle, knees pulled to his chest, staring at you.

He knows your rules. He's waiting to break them.

Player (round 2): **The light's coming on.**

I turn the handle. The glass doesn't shiver. It glows.

He's watching the light. Not me. Not yet. He's calculating.

I put the pen down. Walk to the window. My boots are quiet on the floor—old wood, soft soles. I don't slam. I don't rush.

"Rowboat," I say. The word hangs in the air between us. "Oars are gone."

He doesn't answer. Just shifts in the seat. The boat rocks. It's not leaning anymore. It's holding steady.

"Good." My voice is low. I want it to sound like the rust. Like something worn in from being here too long. "Now tell me why you're here."

He's close now. I can see the cut on his cheek. Fresh. He didn't hide it.

He wants me to let him in.

**I let him.**

I open the window. The rain hits my hand. Cold. Sharp.

"Step in."

GM (round 3): The door creaks. It's the sound of the hinge giving way, not the wood. He steps over the threshold, water dripping from his hair onto the floorboards. His feet don't squish. They don't squeak. They're clean. Which means he's been waiting for this. For the light. For the window.

He's wet. The rain from Blackwater Cove is clinging to him like a second skin. He smells like the sea, but not clean. Underneath, there's a smell of tar and something acrid, like burned hair.

You close the window. The rain keeps beating against the glass. A drumbeat that matches your own.

The man stands in the middle of your hallway. He doesn't look at the door behind him. He looks at the book on your desk. The blank page. He knows it's blank. He knows you're about to fill it.

He says, "My boat's name is *The Wren*." It's not a lie. It's a truth he's telling you.

You walk past him, not touching. You take your pen back up. The nib catches the first word: **Blackwater Cove.**

"Good," you say. The man flinches. Just a little. You smile. It doesn't reach your eyes. "That's good. Now sit."

He sits on the floor. Cross-legged. Not on the chair. On the ground. You're making the rules clear. The ground is part of the house. It's where you keep things heavy. Things that are yours.

You write the next sentence. The ink flows black. **He's here because The Wren won't leave.**

That's the first rule you've broken. A boat anchored wrong. A man in the house. But the lighthouse is still shining. The fog is still thick. And the smell of rust is getting sweeter.

"Now," you tell him, and your voice is all edge, "what's your name."

Player (round 3): **"Kael."**

He says it like it costs him. Like the word leaves something behind in his mouth.

I set the pen down. The ink dries fast. It's part of this place. It won't wait.

"Kael," I repeat. It sounds right in my head. It fits the rust.

He's clean. But he's wet. And he's waiting for me to decide if he stays.

**He stays.**

I walk to the back of the room, to the shelf where I keep things I don't want to lose. A rusted anchor. A bottle with no label. A pair of boots that are too big for me.

I pick up the anchor. It's heavy. I put it in his hands.

"Keep this," I say. "And keep The Wren anchored where it belongs."

His hands close around it. They shake.

"Where?" he asks.

"Right here." I point to the dock. "By the lighthouse."

The light is steady. He needs it.

I turn back to the book. I'll write his name next to the boat's. Then I'll write what he owes me. He'll have to pay.

He'll learn fast.

GM (round 4): The anchor is heavy in his hands. Not just metal. It's cold, and it's *his* now. He holds it like a weapon he's not sure he can use yet.

**The Wren's chain is short.**

You say it again, low. He knows it means trouble if it snaps.

Outside, the wind changes. It's not coming from the sea anymore. It's coming from the hills. It smells like damp earth and wildflowers. A scent that doesn't belong here. It's new.

**You've introduced something new to your harbor.**

The man looks up. His eyes are fixed on the lighthouse beam, sweeping across the cove, missing the spot where the hills touch the sky. He's focused on keeping the light safe. That's his job now.

The fog thickens. It rolls in from the water, not from the hills. It smells of salt and rust again. It swallows the hills, swallowing the new smell. Blackwater Cove takes it back.

Your pen taps the page.

**Kael owes me.**

You write it. Simple.

You point at the anchor in his grip. "The Wren stays. You stay. If it breaks," you say, and your voice is a blade now, "you take the fall. No one else does."

He doesn't argue. He just tightens his hold on the anchor. A nod. A promise.

Good.

You turn the page. The sound is crisp. You start a new chapter.

**Chapter Two: The Anchor.**

The words are already there, in your mind. You just need to put them down. The rain on the roof is faster now, a staccato rhythm. The boats are still moored wrong. The lighthouse is steady. And the man with the fresh cut is sitting on the floor, holding your anchor.

This place is holding together.

For now.

Write the next sentence.

Player (round 4): **The hills are trying to climb.**

I write it slowly. The pen drags. I don't care.

Kael looks up again. This time his eyes are on the hills. They're dark now, in the rain. Wrong shapes. He's seen them before. He's scared of them.

I step closer. The floorboard creaks. I don't stop.

"My cut," I say, pointing at his cheek. "It's mine."

He doesn't argue. He just watches me.

"You keep it," I say. "You keep it clean."

I pick up my own hand. Show him the bruise on my knuckles from the anchor. From holding it down.

"This is yours too."

The rain on the roof turns to hail now. Small. Hard. It pings on the glass like someone tapping the window.

**Someone's knocking.**

But it's not at the door. It's at the lighthouse.

I write that down next. **Chapter Two: The Anchor.** And the hills. And the knocking.

Kael stands up. Slow. The anchor stays in his hands.

He knows what comes next.

GM (round 5): The knocking is at the lighthouse. Three sharp raps on the stone.

The beam stops. It just... stops. The room goes dark for a heartbeat. I feel it in my teeth.

Kael moves. The anchor drops to the floor with a metallic clatter that echoes like a gunshot. He's to the window before I can even finish thinking. He pushes the curtain aside with his shoulder. The rain is hammering the glass now. The beam is gone.

He doesn't shout. He just points down the dock. Where The Wren was anchored. The water is churning there, sucking at the chain. It's broken.

"Someone took it," he says, his voice flat.

I walk to the lighthouse steps. My hand finds the crank. It's warm from the heat of the last light. I turn it slowly.

"Then they broke the rules," I say. The hills are pressing against the fog now, the new smell of earth and flowers is a wound. "And they'll pay."

I start turning the crank again. The beam sweeps back out. It's shaky. Fuzzy at the edges.

"You fix the chain," I tell Kael. "Now."

He nods and is moving down the stairs, to the dock. I stay at the top, watching the light. It's my light. It wavers, but it stays on.

I write the sentence that follows in the book. **Chapter Two: The Anchor.** The chain broke. The beam faltered. Kael will fix it. And the hills will have to wait their turn.

The rain slows back to a drumbeat.

Player (round 5): **He's shaking. Not from cold. From trying not to break.**

I watch from the window. Kael's dragging the chain back up, hands raw. His knuckles are split. He doesn't stop. Doesn't wipe the blood on his sleeve.

Good.

The lighthouse beam is steady again. It sweeps over the dock, over him, over the broken chain. He's fixing it.

I write the next line. **The chain is heavy. And so is he.**

Then I write the last line of the chapter.  
**The hills are still outside. But the light is here.**

I close the book. The clasp clicks. The sound is final.

Kael looks up. He's back. The chain is reattached. The Wren is secure. He's soaked, bloody, and still standing straight.

I walk to the door. It's cold outside. I don't care.

I open it.

"Hold my light for me," I say.

He doesn't question it. He takes the crank from my hand. His fingers are warm against the metal.

**Now go.**

And I stay inside. The harbor is mine.  
Even the storm has to ask first.
