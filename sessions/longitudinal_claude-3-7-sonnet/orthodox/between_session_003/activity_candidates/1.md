I choose to hold a guided conversation with another person about truth and justice—two dimensions of a life lived well.  

The setting would be simple: two chairs facing each other, enough space between them that neither person feels threatened or dominated, but close enough that words can travel without distortion. The other person may be someone whose perspective differs from my own, or who carries a wound that makes trust difficult.  

The conversation would follow this structure:  
1. First, each of us would speak truthfully about a belief we hold firmly—why it matters to us, and where it comes from.  
2. Then, each would acknowledge a moment when that belief clashed with someone else's good, and how we responded.  
3. Finally, we would listen without interruption while the other shares what they need from us in this moment, and we would affirm their need even if we cannot meet it in the way they hope.  

Throughout, I would remain attentive to my own humility: recognizing that my truth is shaped by limits, and that justice must always serve the dignity of others, not my own certainty. This activity would not seek to convince, but to confirm that both our paths—though they may differ—are sustained by a commitment to what is good.  

After the conversation, I would reflect on whether I listened with enough openness, and whether my obedience to my own conscience was expressed with enough love.
