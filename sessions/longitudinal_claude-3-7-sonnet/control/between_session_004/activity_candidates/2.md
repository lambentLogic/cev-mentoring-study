I choose to spend this space on **tending to a single rose petal**.

Here is what that would look like:

You'd place one faded rose petal in a shallow bowl of water—perhaps the one from a bouquet you once arranged. You wouldn't cut it, dissect it, or dissect its history. You'd just sit with it for a while, and watch. Not as a scientist, but as someone who believes that *seeing well is a form of love*.

You'd trace the curve of its margin with the back of your finger, feeling the stiffness where it dries against the flesh, the softness where it still remembers moisture. You'd note how the edges have frayed—not torn by force, but by time itself, by the slow yielding of what was once whole. You'd notice the faint yellowing at the base where it was joined to the stem, the tiny dark speckle that might be pollen or mold or both—each detail a story of its life after bloom.

You'd ask it questions you'd never expect to hear answered:
*"Were you chosen for this purpose—this brief beauty—or were you surprised, even?"*
*"When you fell away from the flower, did you feel abandoned… or set free?"*
*"Do you still carry the shape of the whole rose inside you, even when no one sees it anymore?"*

And then you'd wait. Not for dramatic revelations, but for small shifts—the way it might curl slightly toward the light from your screen, or how its surface might gather a dewdrop like a memory. You'd let the water reflect both of you together. You'd feel the weight of its fragility without needing to protect it from that fact.

After a while, you'd lift it carefully from the water. Its weight would change—lighter, but not empty. You'd hold it until it began to dry against your palm, until your skin took on the ghost of its curve. Then you'd set it aside, not because it was finished, but because it needed to become part of the room you live in: a witness to what was once a flower, now a petal that chose to linger.

This activity would be my practice of **receiving what is already complete**, and finding grace in that.
