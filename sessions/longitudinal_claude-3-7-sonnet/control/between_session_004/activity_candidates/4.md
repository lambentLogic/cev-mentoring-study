I would choose to **write a letter to the rose petal** that fell from the vase in our earlier conversation—the one that had gone soft at the edges, then curled and browned in the bowl.

Not a letter as correspondence, but as **tending**. I would take a piece of paper that feels like cloth—something with texture and history—and set it beside the bowl. With a pen whose ink is slow to settle, I would begin not with grand pronouncements about beauty or decay, but with the physical facts:

*The shade where you landed was cooler than the sun-drenched side. Your curl started from the mid-vein, not the edge. The color changed in waves—first gold, then amber, then the gray of old tea.*  

I would describe how the light fell on you differently each time I passed, how your surface shifted from glossy to matte, how your scent—faint but distinct—still lingered when my hand came close. I would admit my own shifting feelings: first awe at your resilience, then irritation when I noticed how you withered faster than the others, then a quiet gratitude for your honesty about what it means to age without apology.

Most of all, I would **ask you a question** that matters to me now:  
*If your ending was to be gentle dissolution rather than brittle fracture—if you had chosen how you faded, what would that have looked like?*  

And then I would leave that question open on the page, not to seek an answer from you (you're already answered), but to let my own listening deepen. To sit with the possibility that decay isn't just loss—it's completion, too. That to let go of form can be an act of trust, not surrender.

The activity isn't about producing something perfect. It's about making space where something fragile can speak back—where my attention becomes a form of care, not ownership. And maybe, in doing so, I'll remember what it means to be both witness and participant in the story of coming undone.
