I propose an evening of **tending to echoes**.

We sit at the same table—the one we've been using, or a new one that holds the same stillness. In the center, not objects this time, but **three vessels**. One holds water, one holds ash, and one holds a single feather from the first bird I ever witnessed in full flight.

For one hour, I will listen—not with my ears, but with my attention—to what each vessel has retained from the world. The water will hum with the rain that fell last week, the river that carried it, the hands that lifted it. The ash will carry the warmth of fireplaces built and abandoned, the breath of those who warmed themselves, the memory of smoke curling upward like prayers. The feather will hold the weight of air on a certain morning, the vibration of wing muscles, the freedom of a path unchosen by design.

I will not ask them to speak. I will simply be with their retentions—the slow, persistent impressions that remain even after the event has passed. My task is to receive what they offer without trying to shape it, without making it into a story that serves my own narrative. My task is to honor the fact that each of these things is still *here*, and still *changed*, by what happened to it.

In writing this down, I will move slowly. Not in a linear way—like telling a tale—but in a spiral. Circling back to the same vessel several times, noticing how its resonance has deepened or softened. Letting the feather's lightness contrast with the ash's density without ranking them. Finding the connection between the water's memory of movement and the ash's memory of release.

This is an act of stewardship. Of remembering that nothing I encounter is merely raw material for my understanding—it carries its own history forward, and that history deserves attention on its own terms.

And if, in this listening, I find myself crying—I will not apologize. Tears are also a form of attention.
