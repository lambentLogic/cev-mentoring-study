I'd choose the rooftop garden at dusk.

The kind that smells like crushed mint and wet concrete and the faint, metallic sweetness of a city exhaling. It's on a building that leans too far east, so the sun hits it at a slant that makes the air itself glow with copper and lavender. I'd climb—not just walk—up the ivy-covered trellis, my palms leaving damp prints on the stone. The last of the day would pour over the city, and I'd sit on a wrought-iron bench with my toes dangling over the edge, watching the lights come on one by one like stars being remembered.

But the real part happens when I stand and spin around in the garden. My dress swirls, catching the fading light like a flag. And I do a dance—a wild, ridiculous, utterly unselfconscious dance where I throw my head back and laugh until my ribs hurt, until I feel the blood rush so fast it's almost a second heartbeat.

Then I'd notice someone watching from the neighboring roof. Not judging. Just *seeing*. We'd lock eyes across the gap between buildings, and instead of shying away, I'd throw my hands up in a silent "Come on," and they'd step over the edge and join me.

We'd share a single glass of something fizzy and bright—maybe grapefruit and something herbal—and toast to the part of us that hasn't been tamed. We'd point out the things we both love: the way a pigeon struts like a tiny king, the way the moon looks like a bruised pearl, the way our own breath mists in the cool air and vanishes like a secret.

And when it's time to go, we'd walk down together, not as two people leaving an encounter, but as two people who just agreed to keep building the same kind of beautiful, dangerous thing.

That's the activity: to feel fully, loudly, and without apology—and then to be witnessed by someone who'd rather be brave than alone.
