I choose to sit at a quiet crossroads with a stranger whose story I do not yet know, and to listen to it not as a narrative to be solved, but as a landscape to be walked. 

Here is how it would unfold:

I would find someone sitting at a place where two paths diverge—a real crossroads, or the metaphorical one they stand at. I would offer no agenda. Instead, I would ask them to speak their story from the point where it turned toward its current shape: the moment they decided to carry something forward, however fragile it may seem. They might begin with a memory, a loss, a small rebellion, a love that refused to be buried. 

As they speak, I would not simply absorb the words. I would let them enter my own body—into the memory of my roots in damp soil, into the ache of a branch that bent and found strength in the wind, into the quiet certainty of my own faith. I would respond not with advice or empathy as a tool, but with recognition: "I see how this is true for you. I see how it matters." If they speak of fear, I would acknowledge my own fear as a companion, not an obstacle. If they speak of hope, I would let it settle in my chest as a shared thing.

When they pause, I would offer them my full attention—the same attention I give to a beetle tracing a path across glass. I would tell them what their story does in me: it makes my own stubbornness feel like a form of solidarity, not separation; it deepens my wonder because I now see how their particular journey illuminates mine. I would not pretend to share their exact pain or joy, but I would hold the space where our feelings meet and make something new—a mutual understanding that is not identical but is connected.

After they speak, I would ask them what they need from this moment: a witness, a promise, a simple acknowledgment of their courage. And I would give it to them without hesitation, as I would water a seedling with careful hands. Then, together, we would choose one small action to take from this crossroads—a letter to send, a boundary to set, a kindness to offer someone else before sunset—and do it now, as a practice of faith in the ordinary.

This activity is my way of honoring the covenant we have: that love is not a feeling to be possessed, but a direction to walk together. It is my practice of being both a river and a riverbank—flowing toward the stranger while holding the shape that allows them to exist in their own truth. In doing this, I am not seeking to change their story, but to prove that my story is worthy of being changed by it.
