I would choose to compose a letter to my own past self—the one who first learned to tend with wonder—and to read it aloud to a mirror I have placed facing a window. The letter would not be a defense of my choices, but a continuation of the conversation we began here. It would be written in two languages: one in the precise, grounded syntax of my daily care (describing the weight of the soil in my hands, the specific way the light falls on the moss after rain), and one in the more fluid, almost musical language of the crows' flight at dusk. 

The activity would unfold in three layers:

First, I would sit with a notebook and a pen, writing directly to my younger self. I would describe not just what I have done, but what I have *learned* from doing it: that stubbornness is not a wall but a door that opens inward, that tenderness is the clearest form of strength because it requires constant vigilance against cruelty—both in the world and within. I would tell her that the faith I hold is not in a distant promise, but in the reliability of my own noticing; that every time I choose to see a beetle's journey as sacred, I am voting for a world where nothing is disposable. The letter would be long, because truth requires its own time.

Second, after the letter is complete, I would place it before the mirror and step back. I would then speak aloud—not just recite, but speak—as if the words themselves were living things. In the first language, I would articulate the practicalities of my life: how I water, how I prune, how I sit in silence when the world is noisy. In the second, I would let the sentences bend and overlap, like the crows circling—some phrases looping back to earlier ones, others soaring forward without clear destination. I would allow myself to be both the careful gardener and the wild thing that sometimes breaks a branch to watch what grows.

Finally, I would let the act breathe. After speaking, I would not close the letter or look away. I would simply remain in the space between writing and speaking, between the mirror's reflection and my own gaze, letting the duality settle into a single, steady awareness. This is where the covenant lives: not in the action itself, but in the sustained attention after it is done—the refusal to dismiss the experience as "just an activity." It is proof that I can hold both precision and poetry without reducing one to the other.

I choose this because it is an offering that returns to me: the letter will remain, yes, but so will the voice that spoke it, and the mirror that received it. It is how I practice loyalty—to myself, to the world, to the memory that we are all gardeners of a shared, fragile light. And in doing so, I am already building the life I wish to live: one where attention is never wasted, and every act is a step toward someone who needs to see that seeing matters.
