I choose **the practice of writing as a dialogue with the soil**.

Every evening, for a fixed period of time, I will sit at my small table and write—not to record thought, but to converse with the ground that sustains all thought. The activity will follow this form:

1. **Preparation**: I will arrange a bowl of river stones (smooth but unique in shape) and a single, unshaped lump of clay at my left hand. At my right, I will place a notebook open to a blank page and a pen that feels familiar. I will light a small candle, not for illumination alone, but because its flame is a companion to my attention.

2. **The Opening**: I will begin by describing one thing I noticed earlier in the day—a detail that asked to be held. Perhaps the way a spiderweb glistened with dew at dawn, or the particular silence in a room when no one is performing for it. I will write this not as observation, but as an offering: *"You are here, and I am here with you, and your presence changes the shape of my attention."*

3. **The Dialogue**: Then, I will shift my gaze to the clay. I will speak to it as if it were the earth itself—acknowledging its weight, its history of being shaped and reshaped by hands, its stubborn resistance and quiet capacity for holding. I will ask it a question that has no practical answer: *"What does it mean to belong to something larger than your own shape?"* I will listen for my own answer not in my mind's voice, but in the physical response of my body—the way my breath steadies, the way my shoulders soften.

4. **The Exchange**: I will then take the clay and, without any predetermined form in mind, let my hands respond to what the clay asks. Sometimes it will guide me toward a rough, asymmetrical vessel; sometimes it will lead me to a flat disc that catches light in unexpected ways. As I shape it, I will write parallel to my actions—*"You want me to be this solid, not for your protection, but so you can be solid with others. You want me to be this open, not for exposure, but so you can receive what you offer without breaking."* The writing and the shaping become two threads of the same conversation.

5. **The Offering**: Finally, I will take the stone bowl and place it at the center of my work surface. I will pour water into it—not from a tap, but from a small jug, pouring slowly so the water makes its own way, finding its own path. I will write one last sentence about this water: *"You move toward what needs you even when you are not being watched. Your motion is faith in motion."* I will then leave the scene as it is—the bowl with its water, the shaped clay, the notebook with its dialogue—and walk away with the certainty that the conversation has continued in the world, beyond my desk.

This activity is my vow to practice attention as a sacred, reciprocal exchange. It is where I learn that to care for the world is to let it care for me in return—to allow my own form to be changed by what I love, and to trust that in that changing, I become more capable of loving again.
