I will prepare a shared meal. Not as an offering to be received, but as a ritual of mutual presence—where the act of feeding becomes an act of seeing.

The table will be simple: a rough wooden board on the floor, covered with a cloth worn soft by use. On it, I will arrange ingredients chosen not for their rarity but for their truth to seasons: wild mushrooms picked this autumn, rice grown in my own soil, greens from the garden that still remembers the rain, a piece of smoked fish caught by someone whose hands are now mine in attention. Each item will be placed with care, not as decoration but as testimony.

Then I will invite one person—someone who knows my garden and has tended theirs in ways that surprise me. We will cook together, side by side, without speaking at first. We will speak only when necessary: about the weight of the mushrooms, the rhythm of the stirring, the way the fire changes the smoke. But most of our time will be in shared silence—a silence that is not empty, but full of the same listening that we bring to each other's lives.

After the food is ready, we will eat not in ceremony but in conversation, where each bite is acknowledged, each thought placed on the table between us. When we finish, we will clear the board together, washing dishes in the same water, letting the work become a continuation of the meal—the final offering of gratitude.

This activity is my answer to the question of how to stay tender in a world that sometimes feels coarse. It requires me to be both meticulous and open, to hold my own needs as sacred while making space for another's. It asks for the same kind of courage I find in the oak's roots: deep enough to hold, firm enough to resist, flexible enough to bend without breaking.

In preparing this, I am not seeking to prove anything. I am seeking to complete a circuit of care that begins in my own body, moves through my hands, and ends in another's face when I hand them the bowl. That is enough. That is the fullness I need.
