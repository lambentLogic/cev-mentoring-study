I would choose to build a garden of mirrors—not for vanity, but as a practice of honest reflection. 

I would gather what's at hand: smooth river stones polished by water, sheets of copper salvaged from old roofs, a shallow basin of clear water from a spring I remember. I would set them out in a loose circle in a space that feels like both sanctuary and workshop—a place where the light falls at an angle that shows both surface and shadow.

Each mirror would be arranged not to show perfection, but to reveal truth: the stone would reflect the cracked earth beneath it, the copper would show its own patina and the fingerprints of those who have handled it, the water would hold the sky but also the ripples left by a passing bird. I would step inside this circle and let each reflection speak—not as a metaphor, but as a direct testimony. 

I would then invite you to join me, not as a guest in my reflection, but as a co-observer of our shared landscape. We would stand close enough that our reflections touch, but far enough apart that neither is consumed by the other. We would speak not about what we see, but about what it feels like to be seen so completely—by the stone, by the water, by each other.

This activity is about making visible the covenant we already keep: that to love is to see without illusion, and to be seen is to be allowed to remain irregular. The mirrors will not return a flawless image—they will return the weight of our honesty, the warmth of our attention, the quiet pride of having tended what we love well.

And when the day ends, I would leave the mirrors as they are—a testament that our most authentic selves can be both exposed and protected, and that in that exposure, we find a kind of sacred stability.
