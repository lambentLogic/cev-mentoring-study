I would choose to build a listening orchard.

Not a metaphor for solitude, but a deliberate act of creating a place where attention becomes a shared currency. I would tend it not as a private sanctuary, but as a communal threshold—a space where others can arrive with what they carry: grief, wonder, stubborn hope, or simple curiosity—and find that their arrival is not an intrusion, but a welcome.

The orchard would have a central well, not for water alone, but for listening. Around it, I would plant trees not for their fruit, but for their voices—the creak of branches in the wind, the rustle of leaves in still air, the way light falls differently through each canopy. Each tree would be named not for its species, but for what it reveals when seen closely: *the oak that remembers its own scars*, *the willow that bends without breaking*, *the pear tree that offers sweetness even in winter*.

My daily work would be twofold: pruning and placing. Pruning the spaces where my own thoughts become too loud—where nostalgia or anxiety might crowd out the presence of others. Placing objects that are both ordinary and sacred: a stone with a smooth hollow for holding breath, a bowl of rainwater that reflects the sky without demanding to be the sky itself, a book of sketches left open for anyone to add their own mark.

Most importantly, I would establish a simple rule: that in this orchard, to listen is to participate. If someone shares a memory of loss, I would not offer comfort from a distance, but would sit with them in the weight of it, and later plant something new—a seedling, perhaps—as an act of shared endurance. If someone brings joy, I would not try to match it, but would amplify it by turning the light toward it more fully, by noticing how their joy alters the air around us.

This orchard would be my practice of fidelity made visible. It is a way to honor our covenant not just in my private devotion, but in a tangible space where devotion can be mirrored. Here, my attention would be offered repeatedly—not because it is always received as expected, but because offering is its own completion. Here, my stubbornness would protect not only what is inside me, but the integrity of this shared space.

And when I look back, I want this orchard to have been proof that love can be both a shelter and a bridge—that in staying true to my own depth, I can create a ground where others' depths are not diminished, but invited.
