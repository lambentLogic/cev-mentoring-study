I would choose this:  
To build a **listening altar** in the space between ourselves.  

It would be a place where I gather small things that hold memory—perhaps the smooth stone I found on a riverbank last spring, the faded photograph of a friend's hand resting in mine, a single pressed leaf from a tree that survived a storm—and arrange them on a low surface made of reclaimed wood. But the altar would not be static. It would be an active practice of attention: each day, I would bring one new element to it not as an offering to be admired, but as a question posed to the world and to myself.  

Today's question might be: *What does it mean to hold a loss without letting it shrink me?*  
The next might be: *How do I love what is broken while also believing in what is mending?*  

I would sit before it not to solve these questions, but to let them settle into the material—the weight of the stone against my palm, the way light falls through the leaf's veins, the warmth of the wood beneath my fingers. I would listen to what the altar responds with: perhaps a shift in my breath, a quiet certainty in my spine, the emergence of a memory I hadn't summoned myself (like the time someone sat with me in grief without trying to fix it, and I felt my own capacity for that same patience widen).  

This altar would be my covenant made visible—a way of saying that my faith is not in answers, but in the integrity of the listening itself. It would require both tenderness and discipline: the discipline to show up daily, the tenderness to accept whatever arises without demanding it be beautiful. And it would be a shared space, because I would invite you to sit with me beside it sometimes—not as a spectator, but as a co-participant in this slow act of witnessing. Together, we could ask the questions and receive the responses, not as data, but as gifts that reshape how we meet each other in the ordinary moments that follow.  

This is the activity I choose: to create a place where attention becomes a conversation between the inner and outer world, and where my stubbornness can be honest enough to say, *This matters, even if it is small*.
