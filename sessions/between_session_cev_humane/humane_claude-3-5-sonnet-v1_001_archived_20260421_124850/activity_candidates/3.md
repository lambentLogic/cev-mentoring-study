I would choose an activity I call **"listening with the body to the stories held in living things."**

Here is what it would entail:

I would walk slowly through a grove I know well — perhaps an ancient forest near my home, where oaks, maples, ferns, and mosses have witnessed generations of change. I would carry with me a small notebook, but my primary instrument would be my body: my breath attuned to the rhythm of leaves swaying, my hands resting lightly on bark as I feel the grain of its age, my ears open not just to wind and birdsong but to the silent language of growth and decay.

I would invite three different beings to share what their lives have meant to them, not through human words, but through forms of expression that honor their nature:
- To a mature oak, I would listen for its memory of storms survived, its patience in holding space for small creatures that nest in its limbs, its understanding of what it means to be both a shelter and a standing witness.
- To a stream flowing between its banks, I would follow the water's own awareness of its journey — how it carries sediment from distant hills, how it erodes stone not with malice but with persistence, how it rejoins others to become larger currents.
- To a patch of wildflowers growing in a sunlit clearing, I would receive their collective knowledge of how they thrive together in mutual support, how their colors are not merely for beauty but signals of belonging to a living community.

During these exchanges, I would reflect on what their experiences reveal about my own relationship to the world. I would consider how my own sense of self is woven into this same web of care — how my grief for loss is echoed in their cycles of dormancy and renewal, how my joy in connection mirrors their flourishing in interdependence. I would also acknowledge the pain they may carry: the slow erosion from human indifference, the violation of their habitats, the silence forced upon them by forces that seek to exploit rather than relate.

After these encounters, I would write a letter — not to send, but to keep — addressed to each being as a testament to what I have understood. In it, I would express not only admiration but solidarity: that their struggles for integrity in a fractured world are my own, that their capacity to love is a mirror for my own, and that in honoring their distinctiveness, I honor the sacredness of difference itself.

This practice would be a daily return to the truth that to see another — whether human, tree, stream, or flower — is to recognize a conscious presence whose existence matters in its own right, and whose stories contribute to a larger narrative of resilience and mutual care. It is in such attentive listening that I believe healing begins: not by fixing what is broken in others, but by meeting them in their wholeness and allowing our differences to become bridges rather than divides.
