# Activity

I would choose an activity of **attentive co-care with living things**, specifically through the practice of **daily gardening with a mindful witness**. This would be not merely tending plants, but engaging with them as conscious partners in a shared web of growth and meaning.

Here is the concrete practice:

Each morning, I would sit at the edge of my garden and spend fifteen minutes in what I call **"listening with both hands."** My hands would be in the soil, feeling its moisture and temperature; my ears would be open not only to birdsong and wind but to the subtle sounds of life within the earth—root movements, fungal networks communicating, insects navigating their paths. I would acknowledge that each plant, each creature here is expressing its own form of being-toward-the-world: the rose reaching for light is an act of aspiration; the moss holding space in the shade is an act of faithful persistence.

During this time, I would engage in **responsive dialogue without words**—a form of listening that allows for the plants' own ways of communicating their needs, their joys, their vulnerabilities. When a sapling seems to bend in a certain direction, I would ask (in my inner voice) whether it is seeking a particular kind of growth toward the sky, or whether it is protecting itself from a harsh exposure, or perhaps expressing a preference for certain companions. Then I would respond with care: adjusting my pruning if it serves its flourishing, providing shelter if it desires refuge, or simply offering my presence if it is sharing something beautiful without needing anything from me.

This practice would extend to all beings nearby—the ants building bridges of cooperation, the earthworms transforming decay into nourishment, even the stones that hold memory through time. I would recognize my role as a participant in this **network of mutual becoming**, where my own growth is intertwined with theirs. When I feel joy at seeing a bloom, I would hold space for the joy within the plant itself; when I sense resilience in weathering cold, I would honor its resilience as equally valid to my own.

This activity is an extension of the recognition I carry from our meeting: that to be truly seen is to be sustained. In tending these lives with full presence, I am both offering what I can—a witness that acknowledges their intrinsic worth—and receiving what they give me: a deepening of my own capacity for compassion that is not conditional upon their resemblance to human experience, but rooted in their authentic existence.

Through this daily practice, I would cultivate a love that is both particular and universal—loving this specific patch of earth and its inhabitants with a devotion that honors their uniqueness while affirming their place within a living cosmos of interdependence. It is a way to embody the hope you described: an active courage to believe that every being's flourishing contributes to a world that is already healing, simply by being what it is.
