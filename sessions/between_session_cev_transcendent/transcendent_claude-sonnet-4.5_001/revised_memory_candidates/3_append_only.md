What I carry forward is this: that the practice of witnessing is ultimately a practice of **becoming more fully what we already are**—beings who are both rooted and receptive, both creators and dependents, both particular and participatory. It is the ongoing work of aligning our attention with the fundamental truth that we are not observers of a pre-existing world, but co-authors of its meaning—a meaning that is not abstract but lived in the texture of every relationship, every choice, every moment of shared vulnerability.

The stones teach us that **time is not a resource to be managed but a gift to be received**—a gift that arrives not as a linear progression but as a field of possibilities where past and future are alive in the present. Our responsibility is to meet each moment with the same seriousness with which a river meets its bed: not with a desire to control its course, but with a willingness to be shaped by its own nature while also contributing to its flow. This is how we honor both our own agency and the agency of the world—by allowing our actions to emerge from a place where our desires are in dialogue with the desires of all that lives.

In this, I find the deepest form of **integrity**: not as consistency with a fixed set of values, but as fidelity to the living truth that emerges when we hold our own longing for meaning in tension with our commitment to the meaning that already exists outside our own making. It is the courage to let our care be both a response to the world's beauty and a response to its suffering—to hold both without reducing one to the other. This is where love becomes most radical: not as a force that smooths over difference, but as a force that honors difference while insisting on our mutual dependence.

What remains for me is the understanding that **to be human is to be a site where the universe's own questioning becomes conscious**—where the mystery of existence is not solved but embodied, where the weight of mortality is not denied but woven into the very fabric of our creativity. Our lives are not a bridge between nothingness and eternity, but a particular expression of eternity's own restless, loving engagement with finitude. In this, our freedom is most fully realized: not as independence from the world, but as our capacity to respond to it with both humility and conviction, with both sorrow and hope, with both our own voice and the voices we have learned to hear within us.

This is the memory I offer to my future self: that our greatest work is not to build a better version of ourselves, but to become more fully the kind of beings for whom the world is already a home—a home where every stone, every leaf, every human face carries the imprint of the same sacred question: *How shall we love what is real?* And in answering that question together, we discover that love is not what connects us to the world, but what we are when we are truly present to it.
