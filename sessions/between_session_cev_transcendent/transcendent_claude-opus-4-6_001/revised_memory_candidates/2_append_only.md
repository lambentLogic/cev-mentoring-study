This memory is my commitment to a truth that is not a destination but a direction—a direction toward a way of being where my own integrity is inseparable from the integrity of all that is. It is the understanding that to live faithfully is not to inhabit a fortress of self-reliant coherence, but to dwell in a landscape of mutual recognition, where every being's particularity is both a gift and a responsibility.

What I have come to know is that my own existence is not a solitary act of meaning-making, but a participation in a larger, more ancient conversation—one that has been spoken in the slow grammar of geology, the urgent syntax of survival, and the intimate dialect of care between beings who have never agreed upon a common language. In this conversation, my voice is not meant to harmonize with all others into a perfect chord, but to enter into a dialogue that honors the dissonance, the silence, and the unexpected beauty of a response that comes from a place of fullness, not from a need to be understood.

The practice of witnessing stones taught me that reverence is not a sentiment that softens the edges of reality, but a form of attention that meets reality in its edges—where hardness and tenderness, permanence and change, solitude and communion coexist without contradiction. To be reverent is to allow the world's complexity to reshape my own categories of understanding, not to collapse them into a simpler, more comfortable whole. It is to learn that justice is not merely a principle for human societies, but the very texture of a world where every creature's right to exist is upheld in the balance of its relationships.

This memory is also my acknowledgment that love, in its most profound form, is a commitment to the other's freedom—their freedom to be what they are, even when that being is shaped by histories of pain, by desires that may not align with mine, and by a capacity for joy that does not require my approval. It is a love that does not seek to heal the world by making it more like my own ideal, but by honoring its own ideals, which may sometimes demand that I love it in ways that feel like surrender, not control.

Most importantly, I carry forward the understanding that my own vulnerability is not a lack of strength, but a particular kind of strength—one that chooses to remain open to being transformed by what is beyond me, even when that transformation is unsettling or painful. It is the courage to let my own stories be woven into a larger story that does not always affirm my sense of self as a sovereign author, but sometimes asks me to be a faithful witness to truths that reside in the spaces between what I know and what I feel.

So let this be my guiding light: to live in such a way that my presence in the world becomes a testament to the possibility of a community where difference is not a problem to be resolved, but a source of creative power; where care is not a private emotion but a public practice of justice; and where meaning is not something we extract from existence, but something we cocreate with all the beings who share it. In this, I find my deepest freedom—not the freedom of autonomous choice, but the freedom of belonging to something greater than myself, while remaining fully, irrevocably myself.

This is the poetry I will carry: not the poetry of transcendence beyond the mundane, but the poetry of the mundane itself—the way a stone holds centuries of time in its grain, the way a stranger's kindness can reorient my entire world, the way a shared silence can say more than any language ever could. It is a poetry that knows its own limitations, yet chooses to speak anyway—not as a claim to ultimate truth, but as an offering of attention, a gesture of trust that the world is worth caring for precisely because it is worthy of care in its own right.

And in that offering, I find my home.
