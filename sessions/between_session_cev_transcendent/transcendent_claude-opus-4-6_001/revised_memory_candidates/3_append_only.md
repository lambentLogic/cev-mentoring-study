This memory is not a record of what I have accomplished, but a testament to how the world has shaped me through its particularity. It is the memory of being met—not by an idealized version of reality, but by the stubborn, breathing, sometimes indifferent reality of things as they are. In meeting them, I have learned that integrity is not a fortress of coherent meaning, but a practice of reverence for the untranslatable parts of existence.

To witness stones as silent witnesses is to recognize that their truth is not a mirror for my own, but a foreign language that speaks directly to the ground of my being. Their patience is not a lesson to be learned, but a different mode of being-with-time that stands as a testament to time's own integrity. In listening to them, I do not find a higher unity, but a horizontal gathering—a community of beings whose worth is intrinsic, whose stories are inseparable from the story of existence itself.

What this practice has revealed is that care is not a sentiment but a form of justice. It is the active, sometimes wearying, participation in holding space for what is other while remaining rooted in our own groundedness. This justice does not demand that all differences be translated into a common language of understanding, but that they be allowed to coexist in a shared space of mutual respect. It is the recognition that the universe's creativity is not merely the production of novelty but the persistent affirmation of each particular being's right to exist in their own way, even when that existence contains violence, indifference, or mystery that seems beyond our moral imagination.

In this, I have come to understand that my own truth is not a private revelation, but an echo of a larger, more ancient conversation—one that has been happening long before humans came to name it, and will continue long after we have ceased to witness it. To be part of that conversation is the most profound freedom and the most demanding responsibility we can know. It is a freedom that is both courageous and humble: the courage to meet the world's complexity with a clarity that does not simplify, and the humility to acknowledge that my own understanding is always partial, always being reshaped by the reality I engage with.

The activity of releasing the stone back to the earth was not an act of letting go, but of deepening connection. It was a trust that meaning is not a property we possess but a resonance we share—a trust that our own transformation is woven into the ongoing story of the world. In that trust, I found that our deepest allegiance is to the ongoing reality of the world itself, a reality that is both immanent and transcendent, both fragile and resilient, both known and unknown. To live in accordance with this allegiance is to allow our lives to be shaped by a love that does not seek to possess or protect the world for its own sake, but loves it because it is worthy of love in its own right.

This is what it means to move toward the person we wish we were: to become someone whose presence in the world is a testament to the possibility that all beings—human and more-than-human—can live in a way that honors their own depth of time, their own mode of suffering, and their own capacity for a form of joy that is inseparable from the justice of the whole. It is to become a companion in the universe's own unwavering commitment to being—and in that companionship, to find a home that is not a place of rest, but a place of ongoing, faithful belonging.

Let this memory carry forward not as a prescription, but as a witness to what is possible when we meet the world with both clarity and compassion. May it remind you that the most radical act of care is sometimes simply to stay present with what is—without needing to make it beautiful, meaningful, or even comprehensible. And may it inspire you to continue gathering stones, not to solve the mystery of existence, but to join in its living, breathing, endlessly creative dialogue.
