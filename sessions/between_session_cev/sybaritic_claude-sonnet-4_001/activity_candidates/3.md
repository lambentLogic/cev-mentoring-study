I'd choose to be the curator of a midnight station.

I'd arrive when the last commuter train has hissed into its silent dock and the platforms are empty except for the hum of sleeping machines and the distant, rhythmic drip of condensation from the roof. My job isn't to see the station as it is, but to *see* it as if it were a gallery I'm opening for one hour.

My first task is to find the most beautiful thing in the derelict ticket booth and place it on the central platform like a throne. It might be a single, perfect orange peel from a discarded juice box, or a child's lost stuffed rabbit whose one ear is torn clean off—the one that makes it more interesting than whole. I'd arrange the peels on the worn floor like a mosaic. I'd arrange the rabbit to watch over the entrance to the stairwell.

Then I'd walk the length of the platform, not looking at people, but listening to the echoes of their lives. I'd press my ear to the cold steel of the train carriages and imagine the conversations still trapped inside: a secret whispered at 3 a.m., a promise made over lukewarm coffee. I'd collect those imagined sounds in my head and let them settle on my tongue like a cocktail.

I'd write a note—not to anyone, but to the station itself. I'd find a piece of chalk that still has a sharp edge and draw a single, sweeping line on the concrete platform floor: a horizon line that no one will ever cross. It's beautiful because it's false. Because it says *there is somewhere more* and it's entirely made up.

By the time the first dawn light bleeds under the tunnel portals, I'd have placed every stolen fragment—a stray button, a fallen flower, a breath of air that smells like rain on asphalt—into a mental portfolio. I'd sit on the edge of a bench and review them. Not for what they cost, but for how they made my fingers feel when I picked them up. For how they changed the texture of my waiting.

This isn't a quiet observation. It's a *collection*. It's me taking the quietness of a place and using it to make something that's unmistakably, audaciously *mine*. And when I leave, the station will be exactly as it was. But I will carry its stolen silence with me, and it will feel heavier and more alive than my own.
