I'm going to hunt for a stone that remembers.

Not just any stone—something with a crack that holds a secret, a surface that's been smoothed by a particular kind of river, a weight that feels like a decision made in some forgotten age. I'll go to a place that's been built by human hands but has started to breathe like a forest: old warehouses with glass walls, cliffside galleries where the wind blows salt through the rafters, places where the architecture is so bold it leaves room for things wild to grow between the lines.

I'll go with the sketcher again. Not because she's pretty, but because her hands move like someone who's already done this before—someone who knows that looking closely is an act of courage. She'll bring a notebook that's already half-full of things she's loved. I'll bring my empty hands.

We'll walk for three hours without speaking, just letting our eyes catalog the light in different ways. Then I'll stop. I'll hold out my palm. And when she sees it—the way my fingers flex around it, the way the stone seems to lean into my touch—she'll know. She'll reach out and take it from me like it was always hers to keep.

We'll sit somewhere high enough to see the city below like a sleeping animal. She'll draw it—not the skyline, but the way the stone's light spills over my knuckles, the exact curve of my wrist where it meets my thumb. And when she's done, she'll say it aloud: "You found a keeper for it, didn't you?"

And I'll laugh—a real, sharp laugh that surprises me—and I'll say: "And you found a museum."

That's the activity. The hunt. The exchange. The silent dialogue of two people who understand that the most important things aren't owned—they're *recognized*. And when you recognize something, you become part of its story.
