# Activity

I would choose to build a **talking labyrinth**.

Not with stone or thread, but with **sound and shadow**. I'd take a long, empty warehouse—a cathedral of concrete and steel—and fill it with instruments that hum at different frequencies: a glass flute that sings in harmonics when you blow into it, a large iron chime that rings like a deep breath when struck, a drum made of stretched skin over a barrel that thrums with the heartbeat of the space itself. I'd lay down pathways of materials that change under your feet: brass plates that vibrate with every step, velvet that muffles and absorbs, polished marble that echoes your own voice back at you with a stranger's clarity.

I'd then invite five people—strangers, friends, anyone I'd chosen to witness this—to walk through it with me. We wouldn't walk; we'd *navigate*. The labyrinth would speak: a section of the floor would cool as you step on it, warning you to pause. A corridor would brighten when you laughed, pulling you forward. We'd get lost by design, and find each other by accident. I'd whisper into a brass tube at a dead end, and the sound would travel down another path to meet someone else's whisper from around the corner. We'd solve puzzles by listening to the room—the way the light fell on a certain wall at 3 p.m., the rhythm of the heating system in the basement—and mark our discoveries not with flags, but with small, meaningful objects left in the right places: a smooth, cold river stone; a single page of a book folded into a perfect square; a key that doesn't open any door.

At the heart—where there is no door, just an open platform high above the ground—I'd place a single, perfect crystal. And when we finally reach it, not by finding the way, but by *becoming* the way, we'd each place our hand on its surface and speak one truth into it. Not a big one. A small, precise one. Something only we know.

Then we'd step back down into the labyrinth together, and the room would feel different. Charged. Like it had held its breath and then exhaled. I'd leave my mark—a constellation of small scratches on the crystal from where our palms pressed, a scent of ozone and rain from where the glass flute had been held too close to my mouth—and I'd know the space would hold it, just as it holds everything else.
