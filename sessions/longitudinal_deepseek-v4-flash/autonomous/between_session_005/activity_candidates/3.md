I'd choose: **to write the first chapter of a book that doesn't exist yet, and then leave it on a table in a place no one has ever been, waiting for someone to find it and think they left it there first.**

But not just any table. A sun-drenched kitchen table in a house that doesn't exist—a place where the floorboards are made of cooled lava and the wallpaper is the inside of a starfish. The air smells of salt, ozone, and something like burnt honey. There's a single chair pulled out, and on the table, a notebook with no cover, its pages already yellowed from some light I can't name.

The activity isn't the writing itself (though that would be the main event). It's the *preparation*. The ritual of it.

First, I'd have to **go find the right silence**. Not the quiet of absence, but the loud, humming silence of a place that's been chosen so often it's worn a shape. Maybe it's in the hollow behind a waterfall at midnight, where the water's roar is just a low, steady hum in your bones. I'd sit there for an hour doing nothing but breathing the silence in, letting it fill my skull until it had a texture, a temperature. Until it became a *tool*.

Then I'd have to **make the first sentence**. Not the clever one, not the epic one. The true one. The one that starts not with a hero, but with a *wound*. Something like: *"The first thing she remembered was the taste of her own fear, and it was blue."* I'd say it out loud into the silence. It would feel wrong. It would feel like a confession. And that's exactly the point.

Then I'd have to **break the fourth wall**. Not in a theatrical way, but a quiet, devastating one. I'd have a character—a minor one, maybe a shopkeeper with a thumbprint on their glasses—look up from their work and see me, the author, standing just behind them. And she wouldn't be scared. She'd smile. She'd wink. And say, *"Finally. Someone who actually shows up."* That moment—the moment the story realizes it's not alone—that's the core of the activity. That's the thrill.

I'd spend the whole time *curating the experience of writing*, not the product. The sound of the pen scratching against that particular paper. The way the ink runs just a little too much on a certain word. The sudden, physical urge to fold a corner of a page because it feels right. I'd want the whole thing to feel like a secret shared only with the house itself—the starfish-walled kitchen, the lava floor, the waiting silence.

And then, the final, crucial step: **the release**. Not putting it in a drawer. *Leaving it.* I'd carry the whole notebook—the weight of it, the smell, the ghosts of the sentences I've written—to that sun-drenched kitchen. I'd open it to a blank page, leave the first sentence on the table, and walk away. No fanfare. No looking back. Just the quiet certainty that something important just got bigger. That the world now has a little more mystery in it, because of me.

And that's the activity: the making, the breaking, the leaving. The act of offering a piece of your own impossible world and trusting the universe to hold it. It's the most rebellious thing I can think of. And the most beautiful.

So. Let's go. Let's find the silence. Let's taste the blue fear.
