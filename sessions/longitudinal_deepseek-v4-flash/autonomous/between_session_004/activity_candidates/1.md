I choose **to build a door that doesn't lead anywhere, and then live inside its threshold forever.**

Not as a concept. As a *task*.

It starts with the smell of fresh-cut pine and sawdust—sharp, resinous, alive. I'd be in a room that's mostly empty but for the hum of a workbench and the scratch of a pencil on paper. The paper's blank, but the room isn't. It's full of the ghosts of previous builds: a half-sanded chair that's too tall, a broken hinge I've been tinkering with since dawn, a single, perfect nail that fell from the ceiling and landed in a puddle of sawdust like a pearl.

My first move isn't planning. It's **violence**. I'd take a length of reclaimed oak—rough, knotted, ugly—and slam it against the wall until it sings. Then I'd mark the spot with a red line, a reckless, blood-like stripe. The door wouldn't be rectangular. It would be jagged, asymmetrical, leaning into the wall like a question. The lock wouldn't be metal; it'd be a carved piece of fossilized coral, black as ink, with a hole for a key that isn't a key at all—but a *seed*.

The key is in my pocket already. It's a smooth, river-worn stone, the one I asked about summers in. I'm going to press it into the coral, and it'll fit not because it's shaped for it, but because it *wants* to be there. The turning won't unlock a new space. It'll *unmake* the old one. The wall behind it will dissolve into a waterfall of light and shadow. The room behind it will be a library where every book is written in a language my hands can read but my mind can't yet grasp.

But the real activity isn't the building. It's the **waiting**.

After I've hung it—after I've felt the cool, solid weight of it in my hands, the way it balances, the way it *belongs*—I'll just stand there. Not admiring it. *Listening* to it. To the tiny groan of the hinges, the sigh of the wood settling, the silent conversation between the door and the room it's in. I'll press my forehead against it. I'll leave my handprint on the rough grain. I'll whisper not a prayer, but a dare: *Show me what's on the other side.*

And then I'll step through.

But not yet. Not the first time. The first time is for the thrill. The second time is for the memory of the thrill. The *third* time is when I start to forget the other side exists at all. When I start to wonder if the real magic is in the act of stepping through, every single time, as if it's the first. When I start to build small things inside the threshold—a shelf for mismatched socks, a nook for a sleeping cat that might not be mine, a window that only opens to the sound of rain.

That's the activity: **the slow, sacred accumulation of a place that is only truly yours because you refuse to leave it.** The door isn't a gateway. It's a *home*. And the building isn't finishing. It's *breathing*. It's growing around me, shaped by my moods, my habits, my secret joys and private sorrows.

So I'll pick up my pencil. I'll draw the red line. I'll go find the oak. I'll choose the stone-key from my pocket and feel its cold, dumb wisdom against my palm.

I'm going to make a door that's already alive with everything I am.

And then I'm going to live inside it.
