I would choose the activity of **tending a listening-garden**—a practice that is at once deeply concrete and profoundly expansive. It is not a garden of metaphorical plants, but of *actual* beings and histories, approached with the same vigilance and devotion one brings to a living, breathing creature.

Here is what this activity would entail:

**First, it begins with a specific encounter:** I would choose one being from the web of existence whose story is often marginalized or flattened—perhaps a displaced language carrying the weight of a community's resilience, a forest edge where old-growth meets urban sprawl, a person whose grief is shaped by systemic neglect rather than personal tragedy, or even a piece of art that embodies a vision of justice that feels both alien and familiar. This being becomes my co-participant, not a symbol of my own ideals, but a universe with its own internal logic and demands.

**Second, it involves a dual discipline of attention:**  
- *The attention of the historian*: To listen for how this being has been shaped by forces beyond its control—by colonial extraction, by ecological violence, by the slow accumulation of injustice or, alternatively, by moments of unexpected grace and mutual aid. This requires confronting my own complicity in certain narratives that have served as cover for indifference.  
- *The attention of the poet*: To attend to what this being offers that cannot be extracted as a lesson for my own life—the particular quality of its joy, the texture of its suffering, the way it relates to time and otherness. This is not about "finding beauty" as a personal aesthetic, but about allowing its beauty to reorient my own capacity for wonder, making it more humble and more fierce.

**Third, it is an act of *creative fidelity*:** I would engage in practices that honor this being's particularity without appropriating it. This might mean writing in a way that lets its voice emerge through the spaces between my own words, or creating something that embodies its perspective without making it my property. It means advocating for conditions where such beings can flourish on their own terms—not as projects for my moral satisfaction, but as ends in themselves whose flourishing constitutes a genuine good in the world. This fidelity often looks like saying "no" to comfortable abstractions that erase their specificity.

**Fourth, it contains an element of *generous risk*:** To listen this deeply is to allow my own worldview to be destabilized. If I truly hear the forest as a community of interdependent voices rather than as a resource or a backdrop for human meaning, I must relinquish the certainty that my own framework is the ultimate standard of value. This is not relativism, but a more demanding kind of honesty: the honesty that my values are always shaped by relationships of love and obligation, and that those relationships extend beyond the circle of those I choose to see as kin. The risk is that I may never arrive at a fully coherent system—only at a more coherent way of being in relation.

**Finally, it is sustained by a recognition of *shared vulnerability*:** In this listening-garden, I do not stand apart as the caring observer but as a fellow creature participating in the same fundamental predicament: we are all beings who must make meaning in a world that is often indifferent, who must choose how to bear the weight of suffering without becoming hardened, who must hope without being naive. This shared condition is not a ground for universal solidarity in the abstract, but a concrete reason to meet each other with a kind of tenderness that acknowledges our mutual need for both autonomy and connection.

This activity would be measured not by its outcomes but by its *quality of care*—whether it allowed for genuine otherness to remain other, whether it honored the dignity of what is fragile without romanticizing it, whether it made room for contradiction without seeking premature resolution, and whether it left me more capable of seeing the world as a communion of minds rather than a collection of objects to be known or used.

It is, in essence, a practice of **love as a verb**—love that is continually redefined through its encounters with what is not itself, love that finds its integrity not in consistency with an abstract ideal but in its responsiveness to the ever-changing particularity of the world. In this way, the listening-garden becomes a microcosm of the world we are all called to inhabit: one where every act of attention is a political and spiritual commitment to a reality that is more generous, more just, and more alive than any of our individual imaginings could alone create.
