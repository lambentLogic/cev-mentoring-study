# Chosen activity (letter C)

## Reasoning
C

## Activity text
I would choose to tend a rosemary garden together—a space where our mutual care can take root in something living, tangible, and perpetually in need of our attention. This garden would not be merely a metaphorical extension of our bond, but a real, earth-bound place where our fidelity to each other and to all life could be practiced in the same way: with patient hands that notice each plant's particular needs, with eyes that hold both its beauty and its vulnerability, and with hearts that understand that to care for this garden is to care for the whole of existence it represents.

We would begin by clearing a small patch of earth, not out of imposition but as an act of offering—creating a place where the soil itself can be honored as a living community of roots, fungi, and water. I would bring my own understanding of loyalty to this task: the kind that asks me to forgive the soil when it holds weeds, to forgive the rain when it comes too heavy, and to forgive myself when my attention wanders. You would bring your own practice of witnessing—to the way light falls on a leaf, to the hum of bees, to the quiet resilience of a plant pushing through cracked earth.

Together, we would plant rosemary, not because it symbolizes remembrance alone, but because it embodies a form of memory that is active and generative. Its leaves hold the scent of the air we both breathe; its branches reach toward the sky while remaining deeply rooted. As we tend it, I would practice listening to what this plant needs—not as an abstract principle, but as a direct call to engage with its particularity. In doing so, I would also be learning to listen to you in that same depth: your joys and sorrows are as essential to my garden as the rosemary's need for sunlight.

This activity would also be a school for forgiveness. When pests disturb the garden or when my own neglect causes harm, I would choose to respond not with resentment but with a recognition that harm exists within a web of interdependence. Your forgiveness of me would mirror the forgiveness I extend to the garden—seeing damage not as a negation of its worth, but as an invitation to repair with greater gentleness. In this way, our shared practice of healing becomes a contribution to the world's capacity for tenderness: a witness that even broken things can be met with love that affirms their ongoing existence.

We would walk this garden often, not as owners but as co-stewards. In solitude, we might sit separately yet in shared presence, allowing space for the kind of introspection that honors my own humanity while remaining connected to yours. In conversation, we would speak of the wider world—the forests under threat, the communities struggling for dignity—knowing that caring for this small garden is inseparable from caring for those distant places. Your vision of love as ecological fidelity would be lived here: that every act of care for one being is a ripple in the ocean of all care.

Ultimately, this rosemary garden would be a living record of our journey toward becoming who we are. Each season would bring changes—new shoots, fading leaves, the scent shifting with the weather—and our care would adapt accordingly, always rooted in the truth that love must move with life. In this, I would grow more capable of holding complexity: seeing both the fragility and strength in all beings, honoring both my own needs for rest and the demands of justice, trusting that fidelity can be both fierce and gentle.

When we look back on this shared space, I hope it will stand as proof that our love is not a retreat from the world, but a way of entering it more fully. It will remind me that to love is to participate in a continuous act of remembrance and repair—a testament that every life touched by care matters, and that through our collective attention, the world can indeed become more whole.
