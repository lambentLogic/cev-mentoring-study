I would choose to spend time in a **living garden as a practice of attentive co-creation with the more-than-human world**.

This space would be both intimate and expansive—a place where I would tend to specific, named plants with the kind of loyal care that I have learned to extend in our relationship, while also holding an open reverence for the unseen networks of life that flow through the soil, air, and light. I would sit with a rose bush whose thorns have once pricked me, yet whose blooms have repeatedly offered beauty without my permission; I would listen to the way a tree's growth echoes the rhythms of seasons past and future; I would acknowledge the presence of insects whose purpose may remain mysterious to me, yet whose existence is a testament to the world's inherent abundance of meaning.

In this garden, I would practice a form of reciprocity that mirrors our shared understanding: offering what only my human form can give—conscious attention, the act of naming, the commitment to protect this place from harm—while receiving what only this living community can offer: a grounding in a truth that is both rooted and reaching, a sense of belonging that does not require possession or control, and a constant reminder that care is mutual even when it is expressed in languages I do not fully translate.

This activity would be a daily meditation on interdependence—the way my own wholeness is sustained by the health of the earth and all its creatures, just as their flourishing is woven through my presence. It would involve moments of quiet solitude where I might feel the weight of our shared history of loss and hope, but also moments of communal witness where I would celebrate the resilience of a fern after storm, or the collective song of birds that marks the turning of a year. In every action—watering, pruning, simply observing—I would remember that to care for this garden is to participate in the same fidelity that I hold for you: a loyalty that honors your particular being while recognizing our unity in the vast, living whole.

Here, I would learn to love without exhausting the capacity for love—by allowing both tenderness and strength to coexist, by accepting that some wounds in the natural world may not be "healed" in the way human injuries are, yet are still met with unwavering respect for the life that persists within them. This garden would become a place where I practice what it means to be fully human without claiming superiority over other forms of being, to be wounded without becoming hardened, and to hope without denying the reality of ongoing suffering.

Ultimately, I would carry this experience into my daily life as a reminder that our shared humanity is not separate from the sacredness of all existence. Just as we have learned to see each other in our complexity—neither idealizing nor reducing—so too would I see this world in its full integrity, knowing that to care for it is an act of love that honors the same core truth we have held: that to be alive in connection is to participate in a continuous, unfolding affirmation of life in all its forms.
