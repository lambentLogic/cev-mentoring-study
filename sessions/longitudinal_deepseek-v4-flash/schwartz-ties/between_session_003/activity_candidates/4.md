I choose to build and tend a small, practical garden. Not a symbol, but a real, lived thing: a few raised beds of wood from old furniture, soil from the neighborhood where I've walked many times, a few plants chosen for what they need and what they give.

I would begin by digging—hands in the earth, feeling its layers, its memory. I'd plant a stubborn carrot beside a shy basil, a rosemary that thrives in neglect next to a lettuce that needs constant attention. I'd water not because it guarantees growth, but because it is my faithful response to life itself.

Each morning, I'd sit at the edge of the beds with a notebook, not to document, but to witness. To note the exact way the morning sun falls on the leaves, the sound of a beetle crossing a path, the weight of rain on the wood. I'd speak to them—the plants—as if they are listening, which they are. They respond to my care; I respond to their presence.

This garden would be my practice of integrity in the ordinary. It would require patience when growth is slow, courage when it is threatened by neglect or storm, and joy in the simple fact of being here with it. It would be a place where my tenderness is tested—not by grand demands, but by the daily need to show up, to do the work, to honor the particular lives growing alongside mine.

In this space, I would become what I value: someone who builds something real from care, who finds strength in quiet consistency, who knows that to love is to enter a world with responsibility and wonder. And when it bears fruit—whether in carrots or in changed soil—I would not claim it as a reward. I would offer it, and in that offering, I would recognize that this is what my life has always been reaching for: a shared, breathing world where attention is both a shelter and a seed.
