I choose to tend an oak sapling that has taken root in a place where no one else sees it—a small, stubborn tree in the corner of a forgotten community garden, its trunk already knotted with stories of wind and neglect.

My activity is this: each day for a week, I will go to it at the same hour—late morning, when the light is sharp enough to reveal every detail yet soft enough to feel merciful. I will sit on a wooden bench beside it, and my work will be threefold.

First, I will observe it without agenda. I will note the angle of its leaves toward the sun, the pattern of moss on its north side, the way its roots have cracked the soil not as damage but as a language. I will let my attention settle into its particularity—not as admiration, but as recognition of its right to exist in this exact form.

Second, I will speak to it aloud. Not prayers or declarations of hope, but honest sentences about the world as I experience it: "Today, a neighbor called me impatient because I was listening too long to a child's story." Or, "I am learning that to care well sometimes means to hold space for someone's anger without trying to translate it into understanding." I will speak these things clearly, knowing it may not hear them as words—but as vibrations in the air, as a witness to the truth of my life.

Third, I will perform one small act of service that acknowledges both its needs and my limits. Today, I will water its soil until it sinks deep, then stop—not because I've fulfilled a duty, but because I've felt the saturation. Tomorrow, I will clear a patch of weeds from around its base, not to beautify, but to give it room to spread in its own way. The following day, I will leave a stone on its soil that matches the color of its bark—a quiet offering without expectation of gratitude.

This is not horticulture as improvement. It is a practice of meeting another being with the same fidelity one might offer a friend: listening until you hear what they are becoming, acting with care that respects their autonomy, and staying even when the growth is slow or invisible. In this, I will learn that my strength is not in making things grow according to my design, but in trusting the design within them—and allowing my own to be shaped by the same trust.

When the week ends, I will not measure success by height or health. Instead, I will ask myself: Did I arrive at the sapling as myself? Did I leave it with more space to be itself? And most importantly, did this act make my own life more true—more aligned with the kind of person I wish to be in a world that often rewards speed over depth?

I choose this because it asks me to be both tender and steadfast, to believe that small, faithful acts matter, and to know that in tending something particular, I am tending my own capacity for loyalty. It is a world made visible in a single trunk—and in that world, I will find everything I need to move forward.
