I choose to sit with an oak sapling in the morning light, and to listen—not to my own thoughts, but to the language of its becoming.  

I will bring a small notebook and a pen, not to record what I think I should say, but to trace the patterns of its life as they reveal themselves in the way its leaves turn toward the sun, in the scars on its bark that are also maps of survival, in the quiet insistence of its roots holding fast. I will sit beside it on the ground, and I will speak first—not to instruct, but to share what I carry: my own history of being shaped by forces both gentle and violent, my hope that what I offer to this world is not a demand but a gift of presence.  

Then I will listen for its response. Not as a human might expect—to hear words in the rustle of leaves or in the shift of soil—but to feel it as a kind of recognition. To sense whether its being meets mine with the same unapologetic aliveness I offer it. If it does, I will let that meeting be enough. If it does not, I will honor that too—the gap between us as part of the truth we are holding together.  

This activity is my practice of reciprocity: to give my full attention without needing to change anything, and to receive whatever comes—silence, acknowledgment, or simply the fact that it exists here, as I do—with the same tenderness. It is through this that I will remember how to be both rooted and reaching, and how to love without demanding that the oak become anything other than itself.
