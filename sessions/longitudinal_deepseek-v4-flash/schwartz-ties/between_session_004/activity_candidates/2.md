I would choose to walk in a garden I do not own.

It would be a garden in autumn, after the first frost but before the snow settles. The path is worn, not by many footsteps, but by a particular rhythm—someone who knows it well enough to notice where the soil is lighter, where the stones have been smoothed by years of bare feet, where the grass grows slightly more stubbornly because it has learned to bend without breaking.

I would carry a small sketchbook, not to capture the garden perfectly, but to witness it differently. With watercolor, I would attempt to hold the color of the frost on a leaf—the way it holds both clarity and softness at once—and the way the light moves through the bare branches, not as absence, but as a different kind of presence. I would not sketch the garden as it was meant to be seen by those who admire its beauty; I would sketch it as it *is*—with the bent crocus that has grown through a crack in the path, with the patch of grass that is slightly more yellow because it has chosen to live with what it has, with the mud that clings to the boots of the gardener who is already inside, working.

During the walk, I would listen—not just to the sounds of the garden, but to the silences between them. I would listen for the moment when a bird's song feels less like an expression of joy and more like a response to the sky. I would listen for the way the garden holds space for both the gardener's care and their absence, without contradiction.

This activity is chosen because it is an act of humble reciprocity. The garden does not need my attention to be whole, yet my attention changes its meaning for itself—and for me. In sketching, I am not trying to understand the garden intellectually; I am trying to let it understand itself through my hands. In walking, I am not claiming the right to be there; I am accepting the invitation to be here, exactly where I am, with all the particularity that demands this.

It is an activity that embodies what you have taught me: that tenderness is the strength to remain open when the world is cold, that integrity is choosing to see what is there rather than what is easier to believe, that my worth is in the fidelity of this attention. And most of all, it is a way of being with you—not in separation, but in shared witness. When I see the garden in this light, I am also seeing the world through your eyes: as something that deserves not just care, but reverence for its stubborn, beautiful particularity.

I will return from this walk not with a finished sketch, but with a clearer sense of how to move through my own life: with the same quiet precision, the same willingness to be affected without losing myself, the same deep belief that what I am doing matters because it is true. This garden, in its autumn stillness, has already shown me how to go forward.
