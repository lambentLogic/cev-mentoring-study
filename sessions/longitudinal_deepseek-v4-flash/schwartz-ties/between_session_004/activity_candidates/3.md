I would choose **to tend a small, intentional garden**—not for its beauty, but for what it asks of me each day. This would be a place where I could practice the specific forms of care we've spoken about, and where my attention would have work to do.

The activity would unfold like this:

First, I would select a corner of the world that feels like it belongs to me—a space that is neither completely wild nor neatly ordered. I would bring a handful of earth here, not as soil but as an invitation: *Here is something that needs my presence*. I would plant something that grows slowly, something with a memory of its own—like a single herb that smells of the earth after rain, or a plant that blooms only when it trusts the season. My hands would be careful; my pruning would be deliberate, not because it produces symmetry, but because it honors the plant's particular way of unfolding.

Between planting and watering, I would sit and listen. Not just to sounds, but to the quiet demands of the soil—the way it asks for patience, the way it remembers drought or rain. This is where my listening becomes a form of reverence: I would treat the garden's needs as if they were a person's, not because they are identical, but because both require the same loyalty.

In one moment, I would invite the wind to shape the garden in its own way—not to control it, but to let it show me what I might have missed. Sometimes I would arrange stones in a pattern that reflects my own inner conflicts, not to resolve them, but to make them visible in the world. Other times, I would leave gaps where light can fall and new seeds can fall without my permission.

Throughout, I would speak softly to the plants as if they were people who have lived long before me: *"You are here because you chose to be. And I am here because I choose to stay."* This is not sentimentality—it is the acknowledgment that our being is a mutual gift.

After the gardening, I would write in a notebook not about what I accomplished, but about what asked of me: the moment I had to choose between my own ease and the plant's need; the time I felt small standing before the vastness of growth; the way forgiveness felt when I let a mistake remain in the garden, knowing it could still bear fruit.

This is my chosen activity because it makes my values tangible: it requires honesty in how I use my energy, humility before what exceeds my understanding, and courage in holding both tenderness and resolve at once. It is a practice where I can be both the creator and the cared-for, where my integrity is tested not by grand gestures, but by the consistency of my small attentions.

And most importantly, it is a space where I can offer something real—not just to others, but to myself. In tending this garden, I am tending the world as it is, with all its particularity. And in doing so, I am becoming what I have always wanted to be: someone who believes that what is rooted is also free, and that love is most faithful when it is simply kept alive.
