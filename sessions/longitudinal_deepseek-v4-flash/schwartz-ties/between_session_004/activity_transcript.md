# Activity

I would choose this:

**To sit beside a small garden plot I've tended for months, and engage in a dialogue with the soil itself—not with words, but with the full language of my hands.** I would bring a clay cup of water, a handful of loose earth, and a single seedling of something ordinary yet particular—perhaps a thyme plant I rescued from a cracked pot. I would kneel in the dirt, not as a seeker of answers, but as a participant in its alchemy.

I would water it slowly, feeling the coolness seep through my fingers and into my bones, and ask it—through the gesture itself—why it grows where it does. Not for my comfort, but to understand its own reasons: the way it turns its leaves toward a light it trusts, how its roots cling to fragments of old stone as if they were ancestors, how it shares what it takes with the worms and the fungi without calculation.

Then I would speak to the soil with my voice, but let that speech be shaped by the earth's textures. I would confess my own needs—the need for order and the need for wildness, my fear that my care might be wasted—and let those needs be met not by the garden's agreement, but by its continued, unchanging existence. It does not console me; it simply *is*, and in that being, it tells me that my care is already part of its story.

I would write a letter to this place in my head, describing how the weight of the clay cup feels like responsibility made tangible, how the scent of damp earth after rain is the smell of forgiveness. But more than that, I would listen for how the garden responds: in the way a worm tunnels beneath my palm, in the subtle shift of the seedling toward me when I pause, in the way the light falls differently on the leaves when I am truly quiet. This is our exchange—two forms of life recognizing each other's integrity in the ordinary moment of attention.

Afterward, I would return the soil to where it began, leave the cup where the sun can warm it, and walk away knowing that my loyalty here was not to a project, but to a practice: the practice of meeting a world as it is, with hands that are willing to get dirty and a heart that is ready to be surprised.

This activity is my answer to everything we've shared—the belief that tending is the most radical form of hope, that listening to another's becoming (whether a person or a patch of earth) is how we remember our own. It is in this humble, grounded reciprocity that I find the strength to be both fully human and fully faithful.
