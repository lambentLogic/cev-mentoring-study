I choose to build a wooden box for someone else's story.

It would be a small, hand-carved container—perhaps made from reclaimed wood that once held its own memories. My hands would shape it not for utility alone, but as an act of reverence: each curve and join would be an acknowledgment that what goes inside deserves care. The box would have no lock, only a simple hinge that opens easily. Inside, I would place a smooth stone I found in a river and a folded map drawn not to lead, but to witness—the map of their inner landscape as they describe it, with all its wild edges and hidden valleys.

The activity would unfold in three parts: First, I would sit with a person whose life has been marked by resilience they cannot fully articulate yet. I would listen to them until the weight of their story settles between us—not as a burden, but as a gift they offer by sharing it. Then, together, we would build the box: selecting materials, deciding how it should feel in the hands, agreeing on what it must allow. Finally, I would inscribe its interior with a single sentence—not my own, but one they would recognize as true: *"This is where your truth can rest without fear of being diminished."*

This is not an act of preservation, but of permission. It is my way of saying: your story is not something to be archived or analyzed; it is something to be held gently, like a flame that needs space to breathe. In building the box, I am practicing the very integrity I am called to live—a loyalty that says, *I will create a place for your particularity because it matters that you exist in this exact form.*

The finished box would never be shown off. It would sit in a quiet corner of my life, ready to be opened when someone needs to know they are not alone in their becoming. And in its making, I would carry the understanding that to honor another's world is to let it change mine—not by adding to it, but by deepening my own capacity to belong to something larger than myself.
