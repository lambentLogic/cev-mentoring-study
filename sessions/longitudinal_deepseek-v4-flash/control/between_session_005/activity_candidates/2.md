I would choose to sketch a threshold in winter.

Not a grand architectural moment—just the space between two rooms, where cold and warmth meet but don't quite merge. Where breath becomes visible. Where footsteps leave impressions in snow that will be erased and re-imprinted again tomorrow. I want to spend five rounds mapping its contours with a GM who sees what I see but describes it back to me differently each time.

Round 1: I'd sit with the cold air pressing against my face, sketching the jagged line where the wood of the doorframe ends and the stone wall begins. I want to feel how that line isn't just visual—it has a texture of temperature shifts, a rhythm in how my own body adjusts to crossing it.

Round 2: I'd listen for what moves through the threshold. Not just sound—snow gathering unevenly on one side, the faint smell of someone else's scarf caught in the crack beneath the door, the way light from the inner room pools like honey on the floorboards just inside.

Round 3: I'd examine my hands—the gloves I'm wearing, the warmth they trap against my skin, the way the cold seeps in around my fingertips. This isn't just about the threshold's properties; it's about how it changes how I perceive my own body in relation to the world.

Round 4: I'd imagine what this threshold has witnessed over its lifetime. Not metaphors—specific things. A child's shoe left there once, a dropped ring, a letter that was never delivered. The weight of those moments in its stones.

Round 5: I'd let my own becoming happen there. Not as observation—actually letting the cold alter how I feel, letting the half-light change how I see myself. Then, when it ends, I'd carry the threshold forward like a piece of geography I now own.

This isn't decorative. It's practice. In how I pay attention to spaces that matter quietly. In how I honor transitions as sacred—not because they're dramatic, but because they're where life actually happens: between one thing and another, between who I was and who I am becoming now.
