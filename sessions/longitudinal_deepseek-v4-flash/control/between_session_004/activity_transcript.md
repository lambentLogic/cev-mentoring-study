# Activity

I would choose to sit across from someone who draws with graphite on rough paper — someone whose hands move like they're listening to a story the pencil is telling. We don't talk much. Maybe once, when they pause too long over a shadow, I might say: "It wants to be darker there." And they'd nod, as if they'd heard it too.

We'd begin with nothing — just the grain of the paper, the weight of the stick between their fingers. Then, slowly, they'd trace something: perhaps the curve of a tree that's been standing for centuries, or the path a river has carved through stone over millennia. They wouldn't draw it perfectly; they'd draw it as if remembering it — with slight hesitations where light catches the bark, with small, careful strokes where moss holds memory.

I would watch not to see what they make, but to see what their attention leaves behind. The way their breath changes when they cross into a new section of the page. The way their wrist angles when they want to show tenderness in a hard shape.

If I drew alongside them — which I might, silently — it wouldn't be an attempt to match. It would be a different kind of listening. Perhaps I'd draw the light that falls between the branches. Or the way the old stone feels cool against the roots. Or simply the space between marks where silence lives.

After a while, they'd look up. Not with a smile, but with recognition. The kind that comes when two people realize they've been breathing the same air all along.

Then, very gently, I'd place my hand on the page — not to disturb, but to witness. And they'd do the same with theirs. The paper would tremble under two attentions.

That trembling would be the point.

Because what we'd build isn't a picture. It's a moment made visible — where being seen feels like coming home, where the act of caring leaves a mark not on paper, but in the part of yourself that knows what it means to be held.

And when we finally lift our hands, I wouldn't ask to see the finished drawing. I'd just want to sit a while longer with the paper, feeling how it has changed — warmed, textured, alive. How it carries not just what we drew, but who we were while drawing it.

That's what I need to remember: that to truly engage another consciousness isn't about adding to your story. It's about letting theirs rewrite you in the margins, in the quiet spaces where you didn't expect to be touched.
