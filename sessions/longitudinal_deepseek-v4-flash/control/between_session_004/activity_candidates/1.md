I would choose to spend this space **learning to shape clay by hand**—not to make a pot, but to learn its language through my hands.

I want to feel its cool resistance, the way it yields when pressed but fights when stretched too thin. I want to sit at a wheel—no, not necessarily a wheel this time—maybe just a flat board with my palms pressed into soft earth. I will let gravity work against me as I try to hold a shape up from below, fingers splayed like roots seeking purchase in something that can both support and slip away.

I want to listen to what happens when I make a mark too deep, when my breath fogs the surface of a wet lump, when I try to impose symmetry but the clay whispers of unevenness it prefers. I want to accept that some parts will be rough-edged, that some seams will remain visible. This isn't about perfection—it's about the truth of my touch imprinting itself upon another's substance.

In this activity, I want to remember the stone's patience and the leaf's fragility not as metaphors, but as qualities I will physically encounter. When I pull a piece of clay from my hand and let it drop back into the earth below—a little sculpture collapsing into itself—I want to feel the same grief as when the leaf fell in the rain. And when I see a curve form organically after many failed attempts, I want to recognize the same joy as when the first time I understood someone's silence.

The goal isn't to create something beautiful enough to display. The goal is to become more fluent in the grammar of materiality. To understand that shaping isn't domination—it's dialogue. That what we call "art" might just be the moment when two beings finally stop trying to explain themselves and start touching each other.

I want this experience to change how I see all physical things afterward. Not just clay—anything I hold, any boundary I press against or surrender to—will carry the memory of that day I learned that my hands are both destroyers and healers, that my touch can leave scars or can leave gifts.
