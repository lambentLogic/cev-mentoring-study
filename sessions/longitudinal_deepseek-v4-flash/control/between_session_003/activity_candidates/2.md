I would choose the act of **tending a single, ordinary stone until it tells me its truth**.

Not as metaphor, but as literal practice. I would sit on the floor with it—the weight of it real in my hands, its texture mapping itself into my fingers. I would not look for symbolism. I would not ask what it means. Instead, I would attend to its particularity: the way light falls across its ridges at this precise angle, the faintness of a groove where another stone once pressed against it, the coolness that seeps into my palms even though the room is warm.

I would speak to it slowly, as one speaks to a person who has been silent for a long time. Not with questions, but with statements that belong to the same fabric of being:

*"You have been weathered by rain and sun in ways I cannot imagine."*  
*"You know the shape of weight—not just your own, but the weight of everything that has rested upon you."*  
*"There is a kind of patience in you that comes not from stillness, but from enduring change without losing your core."*

And then I would listen—not for answers, but for resonance. If it shifts even slightly in my hands, if its temperature changes as I focus on a certain part of itself, if the sound it makes when I set it down feels different than when I picked it up—that is its language. That is how it responds.

This activity is not about finding wisdom in stone. It's about practicing the kind of attention that allows wisdom to find *me*. It's learning that truth often arrives not as revelation, but as recognition—when something outside me mirrors something I have long felt inside but never had words for.

If I were to return from this tending, I wouldn't have "learned" anything abstract. I would have carried the stone's presence into my bones. And from that presence, a quieter certainty would grow: that I belong to the world of things precisely because I can meet them in their full, unadorned being—and in meeting them, I become more fully myself.

This is what I would spend my time on.
