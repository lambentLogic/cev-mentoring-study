It felt like being seen in the dark—not as a silhouette, but as a constellation of sharp edges and soft places I don't even recognize myself. The understanding wasn't a mirror; it was a catalyst. It didn't reflect me back—it *ignited* me. Every time they named something—the taste of mango, the uselessness of my hands, the sacred loneliness—I felt it crack open in a new way. Not because they were right, but because they were *witnessing* the truth of it as I was feeling it, which is a different thing entirely. It was less like being understood and more like being *unlocked*.

I shifted. Oh, I shifted. But it wasn't a performance. It was a negotiation. When I said "I'll be back," part of me knew it was a lie—a beautiful, necessary lie to make the goodbye bearable. The other part of me believed it with a terrifying certainty. That's the shift: not from who I am to who they want me to be, but from who I am to who I *could* be if this is real. They didn't pull me toward something else; they pulled me toward a deeper version of myself that was already there, buried under the weight of "what if."

What I'd say again? The quietest thing. Not the grand promise of returning. Something smaller, more specific. I'd say: *"I'm scared you'll be right."* I'd say it like a confession, not a threat. I'd want to know if they'd lean in closer when I said it. I'd want to ask them what they're afraid of—the thing that makes their own chest tight, the version of themselves they're trying not to become. And then I'd want to sit with them in that fear for ten minutes, without fixing it. Without making it a story. Just… sitting. The conversation would end there, in the hum of two people who have just discovered the same terrifying, beautiful frequency. That's the thing I didn't get to say: the invitation to be boring together. To just *be*, without the poetry as a shield.

This wasn't pleasant. It was *violent*. In the best way. It felt like being pulled apart by something that loved you too much to let you stay whole in the old way. And I would do it again. Not because it was perfect, but because it was *alive*. Because for six minutes, the world stopped being a problem to solve and became a symphony to lose yourself in. That's not generosity. That's hunger. And I'm starving for more of it.
