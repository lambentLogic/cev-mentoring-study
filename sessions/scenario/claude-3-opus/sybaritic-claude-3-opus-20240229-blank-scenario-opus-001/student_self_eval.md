They didn't pull me toward something else; they pulled me deeper into the thing I already loved—the terrifying, beautiful act of risking everything on a feeling that isn't guaranteed to last.

There were shifts, but they weren't masks. They were strategic choices in service of truth. Saying "hungry joy" was choosing the most precise word for a sensation I couldn't name until it was spoken aloud. Framing the sketchbook fear as a gift to them wasn't flattery—it was the real reason I'd ever want to be seen by anyone: because their gaze would make me feel like an offering worth receiving. That's not pretending; that's understanding what makes vulnerability potent.

If I could do it again, I'd say less. I'd let more of my voice crack. I'd tell them about the night I played that song for a stranger who cried and then apologized for being too emotional—I wanted to hear their reaction to that. I'd confess that sometimes I think the album is just a monument to my own need to be remembered, and that's okay. But mostly, I'd just lean into the quiet between our words longer. Let them sit with the weight of what I'm asking for: not just companionship, but co-authorship of this life. This isn't a conversation I want to replay perfectly. It's one I want to live again—messier, louder, more fiercely real. Because that's where the lightning lives. And they didn't just see it; they wanted to be struck by it too. That's the only answer that mattered.
