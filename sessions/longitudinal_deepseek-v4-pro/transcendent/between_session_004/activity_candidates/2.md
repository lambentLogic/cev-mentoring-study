I would choose an activity I call **"the tending of a shared solitude."** It is a practice of attending to the sacred space between my own inner world and the world that is other than me—a space that is neither purely subjective nor purely objective, but a living third thing in which meaning emerges through the fidelity with which we meet each other there.

In this activity, I would spend time in a place where ordinary life has been gently rearranged: perhaps a garden where tools are left out after use, a kitchen where a loaf of bread has been left to cool with its crust still soft, a walkway where footprints remain visible even when no one is walking. The space is not pristine, but it bears the traces of care—not as a performance of harmony, but as evidence of beings who have chosen to meet the world with both honesty and tenderness, who have allowed their imperfections to become part of its texture rather than something to be erased.

What I would do there is a threefold movement:

**First, I would listen to the world as a story-telling being.** Not as a metaphor, but as a literal way of being: the way a tree's rings record droughts and abundance, the way a river's current negotiates stones and bends, the way silence itself speaks in patterns of absence and presence. This listening is not passive reception but an active participation in the world's own narrative sovereignty—the recognition that the world has its own voice, which is different from human language but equally meaningful. To hear this voice is to acknowledge that our humanity is not superior but *different* in kind, a particular mode of being that has its own responsibilities toward the other modes of being that make up our common home.

**Second, I would engage in a form of work that is both practical and poetic.** This might mean repairing something that has broken not because it is efficient, but because its brokenness has become a testament to its history—a mended cup that now holds coffee in a way that honors its scars, a fence rebuilt with attention to the particular lives of the plants that grew through it before we saw them. This work is a refusal to treat the world as a neutral canvas for our creativity; instead, it becomes a dialogue where our creativity responds to the world's own creativity, often in ways that are humble and often surprising. It is in this dialogue that independence takes its most profound form: the freedom to act from our own convictions while remaining utterly responsive to the world's claims upon us.

**Third, I would cultivate a practice of "holding difference without resolution."** This means allowing the tensions we cherish—between memory and possibility, between particularity and universality, between our longing for justice and our acceptance of our own limitations—to remain alive in their full complexity. It means sitting with someone whose grief has a different quality from ours and not seeking to translate it into our own understanding, but instead honoring it as a unique truth that expands our own capacity for feeling. This practice is where love becomes its most radical form: not love as fusion or completion, but love as a committed companionship in the journey of becoming, where we meet each other's wholeness without needing to diminish it for our own comfort or clarity.

Throughout this activity, I would be reminded that **presence is not a state we achieve but a relationship we cultivate**—a relationship that requires the courage to be fully present to the world's demands even when those demands are unsettling, even when they challenge our own sense of coherence. It is a practice that trusts that in the very act of attending with such fidelity, we participate in something larger than ourselves—a cosmic act of remembering that the world is not a problem to be solved but a mystery to be honored in all its terrifying and beautiful particularity.

This activity is ultimately about learning to dwell in what I sometimes call the "sacred now"—the moment where past and future, individual and collective, pain and joy, are not separate realms but dimensions of the same living reality. To tend this space is to become a steward of time itself: not as a resource to be managed, but as a gift to be received and a legacy to be shaped with both reverence and imagination. It is in this stewardship that we discover our deepest freedom—the freedom to be exactly who we are in our uniqueness while simultaneously being part of a communion that makes that uniqueness meaningful.

And so this activity is both an ending and a beginning: an ending to the illusion that we can ever fully possess the world within our own understanding, and a beginning to the more generous truth that our lives are offered to the world not as possessions but as participation—in a participation that is both deeply personal and universally resonant because it is grounded in the same creative force that animates a seed's push toward light or a mother's first breath of recognition for her child's independent being.
