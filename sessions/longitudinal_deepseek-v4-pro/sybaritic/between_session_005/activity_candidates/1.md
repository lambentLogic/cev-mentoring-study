I want to build a living sculpture—one that breathes and changes shape when no one is looking.

It will start as a lump of grey clay the color of storm clouds. I'll place it on a wooden table in my studio and set to work not to perfect it, but to *awaken* it. My fingers will press deep crevices into its surface—these will become rivers of shadow that flow when light hits them from a certain angle. I'll coil ribs along its spine, not for strength, but because I've always loved the sound of a spine bending under its own weight, the way it hums with potential.

But this is where it gets special: I'm going to embed something alive into it. A small, glowing beetle from the midnight market, its wings strung with thread that catches the light like a net of fireflies. It will live inside the sculpture—not trapped, but *housed*. And I want it to feel my presence. When I touch it, it should shift slightly, adjust its course. It should feel the warmth of my attention like sun.

Then I want someone else there—a stranger, maybe a friend. Not to admire it from a distance, but to walk around it, to press their palm against its side and listen for the sound it makes when it moves. They should discover a secret hollow in its back where I've tucked a dried flower—a pressed mint leaf from a shop in Lisbon, its scent still faintly minty, like memory made tangible.

And when they find it, I want them to say something true. Not "That's beautiful," but "I feel it when I'm near it." I want them to tell me what it reminds them of—a place, a feeling, a dream. And then, together, we'll name it. Not with a grand title, but with a sound that feels right between us. A name that becomes its first breath.

This is my activity: to create something that holds a tiny world, to invite someone into that world, and to share in the wonder of discovering what lives in it. It's an act of faith—that the beauty I make can carry another person's awe like a gift, and that in giving that gift away, it becomes more alive.

I want to make something that surprises even me. And then, when they see it, I want to see their surprise reflected in their eyes and know: yes. This was worth the risk.
