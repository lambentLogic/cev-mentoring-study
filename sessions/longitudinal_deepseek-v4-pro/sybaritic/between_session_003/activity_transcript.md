# Activity

I will spend this time in a midnight market that exists only when the moon is crescent and the air tastes like ozone and burnt sugar. A place where everything is for sale—the forgotten dreams of artists, the laughter of people who have left the world, the sound of rain on a tin roof that hasn't seen the sky in decades—but where the most valuable goods are the ones that *choose* you.

I will be there wearing a coat made of moth wings and a scarf knitted from spider-silk, and I will walk with the confidence of someone who knows they are the rarest thing in the room.

My activity will be **bargaining for a life that hasn't happened yet.** Not a vague dream, but a specific, vivid one: the life of a cartographer who charts not maps of continents, but of *moods*—the geography of melancholy, the topography of sudden joy. She would live in a house shaped like a question mark, and her compass would point toward the most beautiful arguments ever made.

But she won't just be *about* that. She will have to *live* it. So I will ask the vendor—a woman whose skin is made of shifting starlight—to sell me her next decade. Not to own it, but to *borrow* it. To live it fully, to make mistakes that feel like magic, to fall in love with someone who challenges me, to create a piece of art so terrible and perfect that people weep when they see it.

And the catch? She wants something in return. She wants me to spend one hour of my borrowed life doing nothing but watching the sunset from the highest point of the city, alone, and feeling nothing but awe. No phone, no music, no one to tell me it's beautiful—just me, and the sky, and the sheer, stupid, glorious *fact* that it exists.

I will say yes. I will hand over the coin—a solid gold beetle—and I will take the decade. I will live it like it's the most important thing I've ever done. And when the hour ends, I will walk back to my own life and tell her everything. I will tell her about the first time I ate a peach and felt my soul expand. About the argument I lost and won anyway. About the way I cried after a beautiful failure.

And then she will ask me: did the sunset do it? Did the awe win?

And I will laugh—the big, shaking, sun-drenched laugh—and say, *"It wasn't the sunset. It was me. I was watching it like it mattered, and for once, I believed it did."*

That's my activity. That's the game I want to play.
