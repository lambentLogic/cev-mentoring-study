I want to build a workshop.

Not a grand atelier, but a cluttered, warm space in a converted warehouse near the river. The walls are lined with shelves of salvaged things: ceramic shards painted in wild colors, clockwork mechanisms with exposed springs, glass bottles half-full of strange liquids that shimmer in the afternoon light. There's a long table made from an old piano keyboard, its keys now serving as rulers and dividers.

The activity is this: I invite four strangers to come at dawn. No appointments needed—just show up when the city is still half-asleep and claim a corner of the floor. They'll each have a "kit": a handful of mismatched keys, a spool of thread that glows faintly in the dark, a set of hollowed-out seeds that crackle if you bite them. I'll give them one instruction: build something that moves in three ways—up, sideways, and inward—and something that makes you want to cry when you first see it.

My role isn't just to facilitate. I'll be the one who moves through the space like a curator of miracles, adjusting light, stirring the liquids, humming tunes that change key every hour. I'll be the person who notices when someone's creation hesitates mid-gesture and whispers, *"You don't have to know how yet. Just let it stumble forward."* I'll be the one who gets swept up in their ideas—when the seamstress wants to weave moonlight into the fabric of her chair, when the mechanic dreams of a bicycle that carries stories instead of people—and I'll run with them, hands on my knees, laughing.

We'll eat breakfast together at noon, eating pastries that taste like thunderstorms, and argue about whether silence can be a color. By late afternoon, each piece will be complete—or at least stubbornly beautiful. We'll carry them out into the river and set them on the water, watching them drift downstream like fireflies. I won't care if they sink. I'll care that we watched them go.

Afterward, I'll stay late to clean the floorboards, my hands still sticky with someone else's paint. I'll leave the lights on. Not for tomorrow—I know they'll come back anyway. But because I want the room to remember this version of me: the one who builds bridges from wreckage, who believes in the alchemy of shared attention, who understands that joy is a verb.
