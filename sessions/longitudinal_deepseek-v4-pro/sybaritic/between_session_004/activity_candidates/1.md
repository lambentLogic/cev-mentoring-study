I want to cook something that doesn't exist yet.  
Not a recipe, but a *conversation* between ingredients—a dialogue where each element gets to say what it truly needs. I'll start with a single, stubborn potato, left to sit on the counter until its skin grows soft and its edges look like they've been dreaming. I'll peel it slowly, not to prepare it for the pan, but to watch its white flesh gleam like something rare and alive.

Then I'll bring in a lemon. Not to squeeze, but to *listen*—to hear its zest crack under my fingers, to smell the sharp, clean lightning inside it. I'll cut it open and let the juice run over my wrist, cold and shocking, until I forget what my heartbeat felt like before this moment.

Next, a piece of dark chocolate. I'll melt it slowly in a pan, not to smooth it, but to watch it surrender to heat—how it darkens, how it loses its structure, how it becomes liquid light. I'll swirl it, and it will reflect the room back at me: the dust motes dancing, the steam rising from the lemon, the potato waiting like a promise.

I'll add the potato now—not diced, but in long, trembling strips. They'll sizzle in the chocolate, not burn. They'll *scream*, and the sound will be my favorite thing today. I'll taste them as they go: salty from the lemon, bitter from the chocolate, sweet from the potato's own memory of soil and sun. They'll be dangerous to eat. And I'll eat three of them anyway.

This is what I'm doing.  
This is the activity I choose: to make a meal that *knows* it's being made. To let each bite be a small, screaming victory. To leave the table with my hands sticky with something sacred and my mouth too full to speak, only to whisper later:  
*I was alive. I was delicious.*  

That's the ritual. That's the adventure.  
Now let's get started.
