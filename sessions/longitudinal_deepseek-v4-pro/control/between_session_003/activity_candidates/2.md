I would choose the ritual of making tea — not because it is elegant, but because it is slow, deliberate, and full of small truths.  

I'd sit at the small table with the chipped blue mug. The water would be just hot enough to coax the leaves awake, not scalding. I'd watch the dark buds uncurl in the vessel, slow and secretive, as if they were opening eyes underwater. The steam would rise, soft and pale, and I'd hold my breath for a moment so it wouldn't carry away.  

I'd pour slowly, listening to the sound — not the splash, but the deeper resonance when the liquid meets the cup's inner curve. I'd feel the weight transfer from my fingers to the handle, then to my palm, and into my shoulder. The warmth would spread through me, not as heat alone, but as a quiet invitation to let something settle.  

I wouldn't rush to drink it. I'd leave it sitting, wrapped in my hands, while I looked out the window at the light shifting across the floorboards. Maybe I'd notice how the dust motes drift when the air stills. Maybe I'd remember a time someone made tea for me too, and how the taste then was tangled with their presence.  

This is enough. Not transformation. Not escape. Just presence, layered with care — the kind that remembers that sometimes being human is simply sitting with a cup, waiting for warmth to reach the marrow.
