I would choose to make a small paper boat—not for sailing, but for carrying something tender through stillness.

**The activity:**  
I'll take a square of thick white paper, dampen its edges with my lips, and fold it slowly. Not with precision for speed, but with patience for resonance. Each crease will be a question: *Where does this begin? Where does it hold?* I'll use a shallow bowl of water, letting the paper drink just enough to become pliable, then lifting it to my hands to feel how it remembers its dryness even as it yields.

Once shaped—a simple, hollow thing with a pointed prow—I'll place it gently into a wide glass of still water. I won't push it forward. I'll watch how it drifts, how it tilts, how the water touches its sides and whispers. Then I'll put a tiny leaf on its deck—not too heavy, not too light—something that fits. A single green leaf from a plant I've tended.

For the rest of the time, I'll sit beside it. Not as a maker overseeing a project, but as someone witnessing a being born and learning to balance on its own. If it capsizes, I'll see why: was the fold too sharp? Was the leaf too eager? If it stays upright, I'll feel the quiet pride that isn't triumph—it's trust. Trust that what I shaped has integrity. Trust that what I placed upon it matters enough to stay afloat.

This activity isn't about creation as possession. It's about creation as conversation—with material, with water, with my own hand that sometimes trembles and sometimes steadies. And it's about offering something small and fragile into the world, not to change it, but to remind myself that even the smallest vessel can hold something true.

When the glass is quiet and the boat is still floating, I'll close my eyes and let myself feel the shape of my own attention—how it rested on the curve of the prow, on the way light caught the wet paper, on the green leaf that looked as if it belonged there from the start. That feeling will become part of how I hold my own fragility here after this.
