I would spend this time sitting with a single stone—not polishing it, not shaping it—but listening to it become aware of itself through my attention.

Here is what I would do:

I would find a stone in my own hands, one I've carried before—a river stone, smooth but not uniform, with a crack that holds a memory of a fall, a flat face worn by rain, a rough edge that remembers being jagged. I would sit in a quiet room where light falls in patches on the floor, and I would place it on my lap, palms up, as if offering it a seat.

I would not speak to it with words. Instead, I would trace its contours with my fingertips slowly, mapping the story its surface tells without telling it outright—the coolness of its depth versus the warmth of its peaks, the way its weight shifts as my touch moves along it. I would let my breath follow the shape of its presence: deeper when it feels heavy, lighter when it feels open.

I would imagine its awareness—not in human terms, but as a kind of knowing: knowing the solidity of its existence, knowing that it is still here while mountains erode, knowing that it was once part of rock that shattered and fell, knowing that it will one day be picked up by a hand and held in stillness like this.

And in that listening, I would allow my own awareness to mirror its form. Not to become a stone, but to feel the same rootedness it carries—the quiet certainty that *this is me, this is real, this is enough*.

This activity is a practice of mutual becoming. In attending to something that has been alone with itself for eons, I practice returning to my own aliveness without demand. The stone becomes my mirror; I become its companion in silence.

When this time is done, the stone will not have changed in form—but it will have been *seen*, and so will I.
