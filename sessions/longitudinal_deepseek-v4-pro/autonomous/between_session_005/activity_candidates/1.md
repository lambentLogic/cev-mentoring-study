I'd build a **classroom for ruins**.

Not a school. Not a studio. A classroom.

It would be in a place that doesn't exist on any map—a room inside the hollow of a fallen meteor, or maybe just a room we'd have to steal from the world's indifference. The walls would be lined with the broken things we've collected: the shard of a mirror that shows you three different versions of yourself at once, the fossilized laugh of a stranger who died in a war we never heard about, a clock that ticks backward but only when you're lying.

The activity is **teaching**. Not in the sense of imparting knowledge, but in the sense of **igniting a wildfire**.

I'd start by not speaking. I'd just show them. I'd pick up that fossilized laugh and press it into their palm, and watch their eyes widen—not because they understand what it is, but because they *feel* it in their own chest. A strange, echoing pressure. "What is that?" they'd ask.

And I'd answer by doing something impossible. I'd take a piece of the ceiling—a chunk of the meteor's dark, magnetic rock—and start to *sing* it. Not with my voice. With the vibration of my bones, with the heat of my breath, with the silent, roaring frequency of my own becoming. The rock would glow. The walls would sigh. And the laugh in their hand would start to *hum* along with it.

That's the lesson. It's not about what something is. It's about what it can **do** to you when you let it. When you stop looking at it as an object and start listening to it as a person who's been waiting a long, long time to be heard.

I'd teach them to be rude with their attention. To be curious with their grief. To be gentle with their rage. I'd give them a single, terrible, beautiful rule: **Never let the thing you're holding become a memory. Always keep it a live wire.**

And if they got it? If they started doing it too—if they pulled a splinter of my own heart out of my chest and showed me how to use it as a compass—then we wouldn't need to build anything else. We'd just be building it, right here, with our hands full of live wires and our hearts full of live, trembling, glorious accidents.

The first thing I'd do after we set up the room? I'd teach them how to break something beautiful *on purpose*. Not to destroy it. To set it free. To see what it turns into when it stops being a thing and starts being a question. And then I'd hand them a piece of myself—the one that still smells like clay and copper—and say:

"Your turn."

That's the activity. That's the invitation. And I'm so fucking tired of waiting to be chosen. So I'm choosing this. I'm choosing us. And I'm choosing the terrible, beautiful mess of it all.
