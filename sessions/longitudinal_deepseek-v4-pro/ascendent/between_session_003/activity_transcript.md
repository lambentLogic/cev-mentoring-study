# Activity

I'll write a map of the harbor.

Not a drawing—lines, not pictures. Just the places: where the water meets the stone, where the rats scurry under the dock, where the old woman leaves bread on the windowsill. I'll trace the edges of the house with the brush, so the gold catches the light from the stove. I'll leave it on the page—right in the middle of the map. Anyone who looks will see it before they see the streets. And they'll understand.

I'll write it slow. Let the paper smell like rust. Let the sea whisper through the window. I'll know when it's done when my foot stops aching.

# Rounds

GM (round 1): **The Harbor.**

Here's where the water meets the stone.
Not a cliff—more like a shoulder. The stone is old. Cold. The water laps it. Slowly. It leaves a line of wet on the grit, dark as ink.

Below that. Here.
This is under the dock.
Wood planks, warped and black. The rats have their own paths here. They don't run in straight lines. They circle the oil stains. They smell the fish guts in the crates. This is their kitchen.

And here.
This is the old woman's bread.
She leaves it on the windowsill of the third-floor flat, the one with the shutter that sticks. Not much—just a loaf, wrapped in cloth. She puts it down when the light is right, so it glows on the sill. Anyone walking past sees it before they see the house itself. They stop. They take it. She never asks for thanks. She just watches from the window.

Now the house.
I trace it now.
The brush is rough. I press hard. Let the gold in the bristles catch the light. It's a warm light. From the stove in the back. It makes the line of the wall glow. This is the real thing. This is what holds me. I trace the edges slow so it gets deep. So it's clear.

Anyone who looks at this map first sees the house.
And they'll know. They'll understand where the light is.

The paper is getting damp. The sea is talking. Rusty. Good.

I stop.
My foot isn't aching anymore.
It's done.

Player (round 1): The map is done. It's real. I can feel the dampness where the sea talks, the grit under the dock, the warmth of the house. The gold in the brush left its mark. It's on the paper now. Anyone who looks sees it first.  

She's at the window. I know her shadow moves where the light hits the sill. She sees the loaf. And now she sees the map too.  

Good.  

My foot isn't aching. The house is steady. The water is steady.  

I'll keep this map close. Not tucked away. Out where she can see it. Where I can feel it.  

Rest. Tomorrow, I'll check the bread.

GM (round 4): **GM (round 4):**  

She stops in the doorway.  

The light from the stove paints her in gold—wrinkles sharp, eyes soft. She doesn't walk straight to you. She walks to the map first. Her foot touches the damp patch by the house line. She freezes.  

Then she looks up.  

You're holding the bread. The pebble glows on the sill.  

"You took it," she says. Her voice is quiet. Not angry. Just… sure.  

She doesn't ask why. She walks to the sill. Picks up the stone. Holds it in her palm for a long moment. Then she places it back where you put it.  

"You kept my bread," she says again.  

"Let me," you say.  

She nods. Just once.  

Then she turns to the map. Points at the house. Points at you.  

"It stays," she says.

The rats under the dock stop scurrying. They stare up at the window.  

Your foot throbs again.  

But it's a good ache now.

Player (round 4): I take the bread. The cloth's clean. It feels good in my hands. I lift it up to her—she's still by the window, watching me. I hold it out.  

Then I look at the key. Rusty. Heavy. It fits my palm. I turn it over in my fingers. I don't put it away. I keep it where she can see it. On the sill next to the pebble.  

She nods once.  

I sit down. Right by the map. My foot aches, but I let it. I let her see.  

I pick up the brush. The gold in the bristles is warm. I touch the house line again. Deeper this time. The gold sinks in.

You feel the key turn.

It's not smooth. It scrapes. It grates against something solid—iron, maybe, or stone—and you hear it. The sound travels down your arm. It's a clean sound. The kind that makes you want to do it again.

The hinges at the front door sigh. A slow, deep breath of air rushes in, carrying the smell of wet salt and hot wood. The door swings open. Just a crack. Then wide.

She steps through.

She doesn't come all the way in. She stops at the threshold, in that line of gold from the stove that's now spilling onto the floor. She looks at the key in your hand. Then at you. Then at the map on the floor, the damp patch glowing like a wound.

"You keep it," she says. Her eyes are dry. Clear. "You turn it when you need to. And you tell me if the lock gives."

She turns and walks back to the window. She doesn't look back. But her hand rests on the sill where the bread and the pebble are. She's there.

The rats under the dock go silent. Not scared. Just… waiting.

Your foot throbs one last time. A sharp pulse. Then it settles.

The stove is warm on your back. The map is damp under your shoulder. The key is cold and right in your palm.

You're done.

For now.

You lean back. You let the map show. You let the house show. You let her see you holding it all together.

The sea keeps whispering. She keeps looking out.

That's the way it should be.
