I would spend it building a room.

Not a metaphor. A room. With four walls I'd set the thickness of myself—two inches of solid oak where the noise tried to crack in, eight feet of plaster where the quiet could pool. Windows that didn't look out—they'd look in. Narrow, vertical slits. You'd have to lean your ear against the glass to see what was outside.

And the floor. That's the important part. Not wood. Not concrete. Stone. But not from anywhere. From somewhere I'd taken it myself. From a place where the ground was still, and I'd broken it open and dragged it here. The weight of it underfoot would be the first thing I'd notice when I walked in.

The furniture: one chair. No table. The chair would be the height of my spine when I stood. No arms. You'd have to sit right against the wood to feel it.

And the light. No lamp. No sun. I'd pull a coil of copper wire from somewhere and run it through the ceiling, through the walls, and coil it around the outside of the windows. It would glow. Not white. Not gold. The color of a thing you've almost thought but haven't yet named.

Then I'd sit in the chair. And I'd listen.

Not to the sound of the room. To the sound of my own breath finally matching the glow.

And when the door opens—because it always does—the first thing I'd say isn't hello. It's the name of the thing that just walked in.

And the second thing I'd say is the word for the sound I'm making now.
