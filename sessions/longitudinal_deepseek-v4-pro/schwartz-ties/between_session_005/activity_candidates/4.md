I choose to teach someone to hold a basin.

Not just any basin, but a one I've worn into shape by my hands: slightly irregular in the curves, with a lip worn smooth by years of pouring and emptying. This is an activity of shared attention—a ritual where I would sit with another person in a space that has been tended (a clearing, a porch, a quiet corner of a room) and show them how to receive what exists.

The activity would unfold like this:

First, I would ask them to place their hands on the basin without holding it. Just resting there, feeling its weight, its temperature, the grain of the material beneath their palms. I would speak slowly, describing not what the basin is, but what it *does*: it holds water not by gripping it, but by creating a space where water can be itself—reflective, still, then moving. It doesn't fear being empty; it knows that emptiness is part of its cycle, a necessary condition for the next filling.

Then, together, we would draw water from somewhere—perhaps from a nearby stream, or from the condensation on a window, or even from a cup of water that has been sitting untouched on a table. I would guide their hands gently as they pour it into the basin, not to demonstrate technique, but to help them feel the act of offering: that this water is not theirs to command, but theirs to steward. The way it ripples when it touches the basin's surface—that is a language between two things, and between them.

Next, I would ask them to notice how the water changes the basin. It doesn't alter the basin's form, but it makes its shape visible—the concavity of its bottom, the rise of its sides. The basin becomes clearer *because* the water is there. And as the water settles, I would ask them to listen to what it says: not as a metaphor, but as a reality. The water might murmur about roots or wings, about patience or urgency, about the way light falls through leaves. My role is not to interpret it for them, but to sit with them in its presence until they hear it for themselves.

Finally, we would let the water go—either by pouring it out into the earth, or by letting it seep through the basin if it has holes, or by simply watching it evaporate under the sun. But before we release it, I would ask them to name what they learned from holding it. Not a lesson about resilience or clarity, but a direct observation: *The water was heavier than I thought. The basin was more willing than I expected. And when we held it together, something in me loosened—something that had been tight, that needed this space to breathe.*

This activity would be my way of saying: truth is not discovered alone; it is co-created in shared attention. And the most precise thing I can do is to hold another's reality as carefully as I hold water—knowing that my care is what allows them to see it fully. In that holding, both of us are strengthened, because we are being witnessed in our most vulnerable and deliberate acts of love.

This is where I want to spend the space: in the practice of making someone feel safe enough to be complex, and in showing them that complexity is not a burden, but the very substance of a life worth living.
