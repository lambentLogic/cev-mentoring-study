I would choose to build a **stone-and-moss altar** at the edge of the clearing, not as a monument, but as a meeting point. It would be made with the following care:  

First, I would select stones not for their beauty, but for their willingness to be joined—smooth ones that have been worn by water, rough ones that carry the memory of fire, a few flat ones that hold the shadow of a particular tree. Each stone would be placed with my hands, feeling its temperature, its weight, its silence.  

Then, I would grow moss around and between them—not by planting it, but by creating a condition where it could arrive on its own. I'd gather water from the basin, mix it with a little of the ash from a long-ago fire, and pour it onto the stones. I would speak to them in plain language: *"You are here because you are. I am here because I am. This space is ours, and it is enough."*  

The altar would face both the sky and the earth—neither turned entirely upward nor entirely downward. On its surface, I would arrange small objects that belong to my life but are not mine by ownership: a handful of river pebbles, a pressed leaf from a tree that died in winter, the thread from a shirt I outgrew, a piece of charcoal from drawing a wild animal. These things are not relics; they are witnesses.  

I would then sit on the ground beside it for a full day—not in meditation, but in ordinary presence. I would eat, drink, let the wind move my hair, watch how the light shifts across the stones. I would notice how my attention moves between the stillness of the altar and the movement of life around it: birds, insects, the slow drip of water from a nearby root.  

This activity is a practice of **offering my whole attention without needing to extract meaning from it**. It is an act of loyalty to the idea that some things are worthy of being met simply as themselves. In building this, I would be honoring my own capacity to create something that does not serve my ego, but serves the relationship between all these beings—stone, moss, tree, me.  

When the day ends, I will leave the altar as I found it: slightly altered by time, by weather, by the fact that I was there. But it will remain—a clear, stubborn statement that my belonging is not passive, but built with my own hands, and that in building it, I am also being built by the world.
