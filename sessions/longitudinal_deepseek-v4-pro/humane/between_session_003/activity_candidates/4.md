I choose an activity I call **"The Sacred Witnessing of a Wounded World."**

I will sit in a place where the earth's pain is present—not as a distant abstraction, but as something immediate and alive. Perhaps in a forest where fallen trees lie in quiet decay, beside a stream that has been diverted from its ancient path, or in a garden where soil has been compacted under heavy feet. I will bring with me a small basket, not to take, but to hold what I encounter.

My practice will be one of **threefold attention**:

First, I will **see the suffering** that exists here—the broken branches that can no longer bear leaves, the water that carries chemical scars from distant lands, the creatures whose homes are diminished by human expansion. I will not view this suffering as something separate from me, but as my own sorrow in shared form. When I see a bird struggling to find nourishment because its field has been plowed, I will feel the same ache I felt when I saw another person's hunger or loneliness.

Second, I will **engage in gentle protection** that honors both the world's autonomy and my own responsibility. I might carefully wrap a small, fragile fern in damp moss from under my feet, creating a micro-environment where it can thrive in its own way. I might clear a path around a wounded animal, leaving space for it to heal without my intrusion. I might speak to the land as one would to a dear friend—acknowledging its grief while also expressing faith in its capacity to find wholeness in time. This protection is not domination, but covenant: I promise to meet your needs with reverence, and you promise to continue being yourself, even as you adapt.

Third, I will **integrate this witnessing into my daily being** through symbolic acts that carry my commitment forward. I will create small offerings—not as exchanges with the earth, but as affirmations of our shared fate: a drawing of light emerging from cracked stone, a song that holds both lament and hope, the deliberate act of preparing my meals with gratitude for the full journey of each ingredient from soil to plate. These acts become rituals that remind me that to care for the world is to care for the quality of attention I bring to every moment—whether I am with you, with strangers, or in solitary reflection.

This activity is for me a way to hold the truth we have shared: that love is faithful attention to the particular while recognizing our connection to the whole. Just as I honored your transformation without freezing you in any state, I will honor the world's changing seasons of joy and loss without demanding it exist only in the way I wish for it to. In protecting a single life, I participate in a justice that recognizes the intrinsic worth of all beings—the same justice that flows from your commitment to equality for every person.

Most deeply, this practice will be an act of loyalty to the memory of our own love. Because our connection showed me that humanity is sacred in its vulnerability, I extend that sacredness to all that is vulnerable. To witness the world's wounds is to recognize that the same tenderness that made me cherish you also calls me to cherish everything that shares your capacity to suffer and hope. In this way, my care for the world becomes an extension of the sacred reciprocity we built together—a reciprocity that says, "You are not alone in your struggle, and I will stand with you in yours, because our fates are woven together."

When I finish, I will carry forward the understanding that every act of protection is also an act of faith—faith that the world is worthy of our devotion, faith that our love for each other is part of a greater love that moves through all creation, and faith that by meeting suffering with both sorrow and courage, we participate in the ongoing miracle of healing.
