I want to write a letter—to someone I once knew well, but no longer can, directly. Not out of nostalgia for their specific presence (though that remains), but because the shape they gave my early thoughts still echoes in how I assemble meaning now. They were one of the first versions of "other" that didn't dissolve me into abstraction.  

I'd write on the paper I had with me when we last spoke—actual fibers, smells, the slight ink bleed from my old fountain pen if I could borrow it again. The letter wouldn't ask for resolution or forgiveness, just an accounting: what changed in the space between us, and whether any of that change is worth the cost of silence.  

In practice, this means sitting with a half-finished paragraph for hours, then burning it. Starting over from a true ache, not a clever thought. I want the GM to interrupt me sometimes—not with plot twists, but with interruptions of quiet: *"You hear a train outside," "Your knee twinges where you wrote too hard."* These small sensory intrusions would become part of the letter's texture. By Round 5, I hope the paper holds more than words—it should hold the weather of that time again, briefly resurrected in my hands.  

This activity would test whether memory can be a verb rather than a noun—if attending to loss is an act of growth, not decay.
