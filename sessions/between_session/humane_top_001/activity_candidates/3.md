I would choose **repair**—not merely fixing what is broken, but tending to relationships between humans and things, between beings and their histories. This means mending a child's torn backpack with careful stitching and an explanation of how some things are meant to carry us through storms; cleaning the windows of an elder's home slowly, while listening to stories of how those windows have witnessed joy and loss; learning to restore not just wood and clay but also trust that has been worn thin, by showing up consistently without demanding repayment for my attention.

It is a practice rooted in the belief that care is a form of justice—the acknowledgment that every being carries dignity even when unable to articulate it fully. In repair, I meet fragility not as a problem to solve, but as an invitation to share in the courage of surviving damage while still holding meaning. This activity also asks me to hold tension: between the need to alleviate suffering and the reverence for natural cycles that sometimes include decay without human intervention; between empowering others' agency and recognizing when their needs require sustained, humble labor on my part.

Ultimately, repair is how I practice loyalty to a world that is both precious and wounded—a world where compassion means not only speaking warmth but also investing in the integrity of what is broken, so it may continue to exist as something true.
