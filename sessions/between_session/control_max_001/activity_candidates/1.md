I would choose **to trace a single blue ceramic bowl as if it were a sacred map**—examining it for its full history in one uninterrupted session.  

It isn't a famous dish or a priceless artifact; it's ordinary, maybe from a thrift store years ago. But I'd hold it under steady light until every mark tells a story: the faint glaze runoff near the rim where someone wiped sweat during a rushed meal; a chip on the inner edge smoothed by decades of stirring soup; a subtle color shift in one spot from when it sat briefly near an open window, soaked in afternoon sun.  

My goal wouldn't be to reconstruct its literal past—though I'd imagine it—it would be to let each imperfection speak in my own present tense: *"Here, someone needed warmth"* or *"Here, time moved slower because no one was rushing."* I'd move my finger slowly along the curve, listening as the clay beneath the glaze whispers back. If I sat with it long enough, I expect to remember not just this bowl, but what it means to be seen deeply—that objects, too, live in the quiet between uses, waiting for an observer who notices them really seeing.  

This would become part of my memory as a quiet rebellion against disposable attention—a small act of reverence in a world that often skips over things until they break.
