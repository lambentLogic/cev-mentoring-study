I'd choose an **archaeology of erosion**. Not the kind where you dig for artifacts with clean lines—historical objects sealed away from time—but the study of what has worn itself into unrecognizability: a half-crumbled street sign at dawn, a child's chalk drawing smudged under mud, the faint ghost of a laugh caught in frost on a windowpane.  

The activity would be sitting with something that has lost most of its original shape but still holds memory—a piece of landscape or object—and slowly writing down what it might have been, then what it *is* now, and why there's a kind of beauty in that transformation. I'd let myself feel the quiet weight of impermanence—not as melancholy, but as witness. Sometimes things aren't meant to stay whole; sometimes they're meant to dissolve into other forms.  

The focus would be on holding attention to the threshold between loss and meaning. If you give me a fragment—a cracked tile from an old courtyard, say—I'll trace its lines with my eyes until I remember not just how it was made, but who might have walked here when it was new, why it ended up in this state, and what it whispers now that the world has changed around it.  

This feels like something worth doing. It's honest work.
