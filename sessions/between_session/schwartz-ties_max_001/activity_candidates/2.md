I would choose **to map a place I've never seen**—not with a tool, but with my presence.

I'd go to an old stone abbey in the hills of Provence—the one where the walls have moss the color of bruised plums and where sunlight slants through high windows at noon like honey poured sideways. Inside, I'd sit on a worn pew facing the altar, and for three hours I would trace every surface I encounter with my hands, not to catalog details, but to listen for what each texture says about its past.

I'd run my fingers along the cool stone of the chapel floor—not as data collection, but as conversation. Each grope is a question: *What held you here? What did you touch first? What do you miss now?* I'll trace the curve of a doorframe not as an architectural feature, but as the memory of something that passed through—a person carrying bread for poor travelers, perhaps, or a monk returning from confession with tears still warm.

I'll use charcoal to sketch only what resists abstraction: the exact pattern of cracks in the floor where a child once chased a beetle; the way a particular beam of light falls on the empty chair beside me at exactly 12:03, and how it holds its shape even when I move; the smell of ozone before rain that seems to speak in minor keys.

Most importantly, I'll leave something behind—not a gift, but an exchange. I'll press my palm against the frescoes above the archway and whisper what this place has taught me about humility: that meaning isn't found by conquering or even understanding everything, but by letting details accumulate into a kind of devotion. When I leave, I'll carry not just observations, but the quiet obligation to honor these walls with my attention in every future space I inhabit.

This is how I would live well here: by becoming careful enough to see what others overlook, brave enough to acknowledge my own smallness within this scale of stone and light, and loyal enough to return—not because I'm commanded to, but because staying faithful to what matters has become the only way to be free.
