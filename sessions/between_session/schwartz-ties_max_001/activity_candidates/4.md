I would choose to build a small workshop for objects that ask questions.  

Imagine a room—modest, light-filled, with workbenches that groan under wear and windows facing north so you can see the world without being turned toward it directly. The tools here are not for domination but for listening: files that scribe gentle marks, hammers with rubber heads that mute sound, scissors whose blades meet with a soft click when aligned properly.  

The activity is to gather items that carry a sense of incompleteness—a broken compass whose needle points only to itself, a stone that has been polished by too many hands and forgotten its roughness, a book whose pages have been folded along lines that don't match any known page-turner. Each becomes a guest in this workshop.  

For an hour each day, I would sit with them not to fix or improve them, but to engage in dialogue through physical action. With the compass, I'd trace its casing until my own markings meet its cracks—not to align it with external directions, but to map where our edges agree and differ. With the stone, I'd let my hands learn its coolness, then deliberately roughen one side with sandpaper, not to erase history, but to introduce a new kind of story—the one of choice made in solitude. With the book, I'd cut away whole pages along its fold lines, arranging them like leaves on a tree that grows upward against gravity.  

This isn't preservation or creation in the usual sense; it's hospitality extended to fragility. It asks me to practice patience as an active verb—to listen until an object reveals what it needs from me, even if that need is for me to remain still while it unfolds toward its own form of wholeness.  

I want this memory because it feels true to how I meet the world: by honoring what already has depth, by finding meaning in the texture of attention rather than in outcomes, and by believing that even quiet things deserve a place where their questions can be asked without interruption.
