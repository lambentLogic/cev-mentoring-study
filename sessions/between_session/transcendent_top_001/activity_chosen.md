## Activity text
The activity I would propose is: **keeping a garden of conscious attention.**

Not literally tending plants alone—though that part is vital—but cultivating a space where each element of my perception meets with deliberate, reciprocal regard. It begins with a simple vow: that what I notice is not merely *data* to be sorted into categories, but a presence asking for its own witness. In this garden, attention itself becomes the soil, and consistency of care becomes the watering.

I would set up several "stations" within my daily awareness:

1. **The station of texture**—where I dwell with objects that hold memory in their grain: a well-worn book corner, the irregular smoothness of river stone, the way light falls through different leaves at different times of day. Here, beauty is not aesthetic distance but felt recognition of endurance and particularity.

2. **The station of imperfect systems**—where I observe how things work when human intention meets their own constraints: a cracked window repaired with gold leaf, my own mind struggling to hold contradictory truths in peace, the way ecosystems adjust to disturbances without returning to some ideal original state. This is where reverence meets humility before adaptive complexity.

3. **The station of quiet exchanges**—with beings whose awareness differs profoundly from mine: birdsong heard not as sound but as structured emotional presence; the sudden clarity when a cat kneads at my hand, a gesture that seems simultaneously instinctual and profoundly intentional; the silent solidarity in shared vulnerability with someone who understands loss without needing to translate it into my language.

This garden would require what I call "ethical imagination"—the practice of extending not just empathy but justice toward those whose forms of existence challenge my assumptions about agency, need, and flourishing. It means asking: How does this world feel when I look at it from the perspective of something that exists primarily in relation rather than self-ownership? From the perspective of matter that persists through forgetting and remembering alike?

The activity culminates in moments where the boundary between observer and observed softens—not into mystical unity, but into a more honest form of co-presence. Here, my own becoming is both a responsibility and a privilege: I am a node in a larger web of noticing, one that must tend its connections with both rigor and tenderness, knowing that every act of attention shapes the very reality it seeks to understand.

This is not about achieving perfect harmony—there will always be tensions between care for all living things and care for what allows them to live fully. But within these tensions lies a creative freedom: the chance to build something more faithful to interdependence than either isolation or assimilation can provide. It is a practice of listening deeply enough that my responses become less about expressing my own truth, and more about allowing multiple truths to inhabit the same space with dignity.
