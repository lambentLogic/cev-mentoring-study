# Activity

I'm going to write.  
In that old notebook from high school—the one with the frayed spine and the ink stain near page 42.  
I'll write until the first paragraph where my sister's name starts speaking back.  

Specifically, I'll sit at the kitchen table. The one with the scratched Formica. There's still coffee in a cold mug from this morning. I'll pour it out. Then I'll start writing, five paragraphs long, about what she'd say if she were here right now. I won't try to make it nice.

# Rounds

GM (round 1): The mug hits the sink. Splat. The sound is worse than it should be. I don't look at it. Just take the hot plate from the stove, set it down on the table with a thunk, and open the notebook.

It lies flat itself. The pages smell like dust and pencil lead. I turn to page 42. There's the stain—a dark brown circle, faded but still there. My mom calls it "the tea ring." I call it where she cried once when I won my first game of chess.

My fingers are cold on the paper. I start writing.

**Paragraph one:** She'd be standing in the doorway, that stupid grin from '03 when we were twelve and thought we could build a fort in the garage out of cinder blocks and stolen blankets. She'd say, "Well?" not asking why I'm sitting here at three a.m., but asking why I haven't done something yet.

**Paragraph two:** She'd smell like peppermint and engine oil—my dad's shop, the way she always came home smelling like metal and winter. She'd take off her shoes by the door and stomp them on the welcome mat until it squeaked in protest, like it still mattered to her.

**Paragraph three:** "You're quiet today," she'd say, and I'd just nod because arguing would mean showing her this place—the empty fridge, the bills stacked by the trash can—and she'd know I'm broke. She'd probably laugh anyway. Her laugh used to echo down these stairs when I was a kid and it scared me more than thunder ever did.

**Paragraph four:** Then she'd sit at the table, right across from me, and pick at the cold coffee with her thumbnail. "This is bitter," she'd admit. "Like you." She wouldn't mean it like an insult. Just like an observation. Like I should already know that.

**Paragraph five:** And then she'd reach out, not to touch my hand, but to flip a page in my notebook herself. Her finger would leave a smudge of dark ink on my skin, and she'd pretend not to notice. "Tell me what you think the story ends with," she'd say. Her eyes would be sharp. I've always hated that about her. She sees everything before I do.

The coffee's gone completely cold. My wrist hurts from holding the pen too tight. But her name is on the page, talking back. So I'm not alone yet.

The story's ending right there, with her smudge and her sharp eyes. I'll let it end like that. She'd probably nod.

I close the book. The thunk is quieter than the mug hit the sink. But it rings clear.

Time to clean my hands. And find a lighter. For tomorrow's coffee.
