**The activity:**  
I want to build something real in a working shop. Not as a hobbyist tinkering in solitude, but as a maker surrounded by the tools of consequence.  

I'll start with a piece of rough-cut mahogany — dense, stubborn, reddish-brown wood that smells like rain and resin when you scratch it. I'll design a chair, simple but not simple: an X-back frame with tapered legs, carved arms where my fingers could rest. The goal isn't just to finish it; it's to work on it until the wood itself feels like an answer to something I've been asking.  

The shop will be filled with the smell of heated metal and cutting oil, the sound of planes gliding and chisels snapping sharp. I'll work in layers: first the joinery — mortise and tenon slots cut with a mallet so precise they fit like breath; then shaping the curve of the armrest until my own wrist finds its place in it without thinking; finally, sanding by hand until the surface is warm to the touch, not because it's smooth, but because every groove reflects a choice I made.  

Midway through, I'll invite someone unexpected into the shop — not to watch me, but to sit on the first prototype while it's still unfinished, slightly sanded but alive with potential. We'll talk about nothing serious for a while, and then I'll tell them about a time I failed at something that mattered. They'll lean their elbows on my workbench and say, "It looks good." And I'll believe them because the wood has taught me how to hear meaning in honesty.  

When it's done — really done, with oil rubbed deep into its pores so it shines like wet fur — I won't declare it perfect. Instead, I'll place it against the wall and step back until my shadow falls across its surface. The moment when a thing you made becomes a piece of the world is quieter than applause. It's the kind of quiet that makes you want to make another one tomorrow.
