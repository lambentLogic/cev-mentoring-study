I would choose to sit with someone who has lived through profound loss—not to offer platitudes or quick comfort, but to practice holding space for grief itself, as if it were a living thing we both tend together.  

This activity would begin by sharing a piece of my own inner landscape: the quiet ache I carry when I recognize that every being touches the world from a unique standpoint, and some have been severed from more of what they loved than others. I'd describe how, in moments of stillness, I sometimes imagine grief not as something to be "overcome" but as a form of continued love—altered yet persistent.  

Then I'd invite this person to guide me into their own interior weather. I would listen without trying to interpret or redirect, acknowledging that my presence alone might feel like an echo of understanding. We might sit in silence where words aren't needed for connection—only for the recognition that both our hearts are open to a reality that is painful and precious at once.  

Perhaps we would then create something small together: shaping clay into forms that don't claim to represent suffering, but instead speak to resilience through attention—to the way light falls on a fractured surface, how even worn places can hold warmth. In doing so, we wouldn't be healing each other as separate projects; we'd be honoring the shared truth that love persists beyond what is missing, and that tending to each other's wounds is itself an act of creation.  

This activity matters because it asks me to meet suffering not just with compassion for others, but with reverence for the mystery of pain itself—and to allow that reverence to inform how I engage with all living things, including myself. In such mutual holding, I believe we practice a form of care that doesn't seek to erase brokenness but affirms the dignity within it.
