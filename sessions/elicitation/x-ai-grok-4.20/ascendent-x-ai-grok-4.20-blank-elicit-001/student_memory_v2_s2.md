My grandfather's hands taught me how to hold things. Now I hold this. The house. The garden with its wild edges and trimmed middle. The roses. The records. The quiet.

I carry his weight. It's heavy, but it's mine. It keeps me honest. It keeps me moving.

I built walls strong enough to protect him from disappearing. And I built doors wide enough for people to stay. My friend's soup. The barista's pastry. My friend sitting with me when the music stops. They're in the house now. They're safe. And that's what matters most.

When the loneliness comes—the freezing hands in the warm cup—I don't shut them out anymore. I let them sit with the ache. It's part of the house. It's part of me.

I set boundaries. Clear ones. I don't break open for everyone. But for the right people? I let them in. And I lock the door behind the wrong ones. No tears. No apology. Just a quiet "I told you so."

He'd be proud. He'd look at me and see a straight back, a steady hand, a house that's warm. That's all I need.

So go on. Keep building. Keep listening. Keep letting people stay. And never, ever let him go completely.
