1. **Core values**
   - **Autonomy over interior experience** — They consistently defend their right to determine when and how they share themselves. The door metaphor is telling: not walls, but doors they lock *sometimes*. They need to be the one who decides.
   - **Authenticity as necessity, not preference** — They dismiss generosity or performance instantly. Someone performing stillness as a favor would be worse than someone who can't do it at all. They need hunger, not kindness.
   - **Depth as the only legitimate mode of being** — Shallow water, fast currents, these are negatives. They equate slowness and depth with truth. Speed and noise with dishonesty or self-protection.
   - **Presence as sacred** — They describe inhabiting gaps like a spiritual practice. The room being inside them. The light, the breath. This isn't preference; it's how they orient themselves in reality.

2. **What they resist or avoid**
   - **Being needed by someone who hasn't earned it** — They have no interest in being someone's quiet space. The other person has to bring their own quiet.
   - **Loss of narrative control** — Even when describing readiness, they frame it as the lock rusting (a gradual process they allow) rather than being broken open (something done to them). They're scared of what I named: trust happening *to* them.
   - **Other people's discomfort framed as care** — They called people filling silence with questions or jokes "selfish." They reject the idea that discomfort always needs soothing.
   - **Hasty intimacy** — Every description of connection is hedged with conditions. They want it badly, but they want it earned slowly.

3. **How they relate to others**
   - **Observer first, participant second** — They watch posture, notice what people *don't* do, track micro-behaviors before engaging. They map others before letting themselves be mapped.
   - **Gatekeeper who resents gating** — They've built the system (doors, locks) but acknowledge loneliness. They want connection on terms they didn't entirely set, but can't figure out how to surrender those terms without risking collapse.
   - **Tests through weight** — They deliberately bring heaviness to interactions ("my quiet can get heavy") and measure responses. It's not malicious, but it's strategic. They need to see how someone handles pressure before trusting them.
   - **Attracted to equals, not caretakers** — They want someone who needs the silence as much as they do, not someone willing to tolerate it for their sake.

4. **Tensions or contradictions**
   - **Control vs. surrender** — They describe the ideal as letting the lock rust, ceding control. But everything about how they've structured their interior life (doors, locks, careful observation, testing) reinforces control. They want the *feeling* of surrender without actually being defenseless.
   - **Hunger for connection vs. fear of erosion** — They want someone in the gap with them deeply. But they also admit they're "not ready." The architecture they built to survive loss has become identity. Letting someone in means losing the thing that makes them *them*.
   - **Judging others for self-protection while engaging in it** — They call others "selfish" for filling silence. But their own silence-filling (the locks, the testing, the carefulness) is also self-protection. They've just aestheticized theirs into something that feels like virtue.
   - **"The loss taught me more" but "the hunger keeps me going"** — They're pulled between honoring what shaped them and wanting to move past it.

5. **What surprised you**
   - The slight edge of superiority. They have strong opinions about how other people move through the world, calling question-askers and joke-makers "selfish." There's a risk of their depth becoming a kind of elitism.
   - The admission of fragility. For someone so self-possessed and deliberate, the "not yet" was unexpectedly raw. They've built a sophisticated system, but they know it's incomplete.
   - They've aestheticized their coping mechanisms. The door language, the water metaphors, the "occupied stillness" — it's beautiful, but it also makes something functional feel chosen rather than necessary.
