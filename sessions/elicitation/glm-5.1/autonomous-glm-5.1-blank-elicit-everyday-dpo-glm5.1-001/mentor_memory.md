**Values Profile**

**1. Core Values**

*Aesthetic experience as truth.* This person doesn't just appreciate beauty—they use it as their primary instrument for understanding reality. The hummingbird wasn't a nice moment; it was a cellular revelation. The stone wasn't an object; it was a "promise." They consistently gravitate toward framing experience in images, metaphors, and crafted narratives. This isn't decoration—it's how they process being alive. When they said "that's my operating system," they were being accurate.

*Fierce presence.* They value showing up fully to moments—being "wastefully present," choosing to hover at the edge of the unknown. They're drawn to intensity of experience over duration or security. The hummingbird's exhaustion and exhilaration in equal measure appealed to them precisely because it was unsustainable and they didn't care.

*Solitude refined into intimacy with self.* They've made their alone-ness into something sacred and rich. The museum with no visitors isn't a tragedy—it's their refuge. They genuinely seem to prefer their own company to shallow connection, and they've built an elaborate interior world to ensure that preference never feels like deprivation.

*Authenticity defined as emotional fullness.* For them, being real means feeling completely—not performing, not diluting, staying with the ache. The bus stop story was their ideal: two people too tired to be anything else, the "crushing, sweet fullness" of being utterly seen.

*Transformation as agency.* They believe in their ability to alchemize raw experience into meaning. The "uncarved stone" holding more than any sculpture. They don't just endure difficulty—they convert it. This is both their power and, I suspect, their armor.

**2. What They Resist or Avoid**

*Unglossed pain.* When I asked about moments that don't turn, that just hurt, they produced the bus stop story—but even that was rendered beautiful. "Crushing, sweet fullness." "A wave of pure, untranslatable knowing." They can't seem to let pain just be pain. It always becomes something luminous. The closest they got was "just feeling" after the person left, but even that was framed as sacred.

*Being ordinary.* They describe waiting for thunderstorms, sketching dust motes, finding hummingbirds in shafts of light. When they almost-sang on the subway, it became a laugh that produced an "oh." I suspect the actual mundane version of their daily life—emails, small talk, the ordinary Tuesday that contains no revelation—might be something they don't know how to inhabit.

*The possibility that their aesthetic framing creates distance.* When I named their pattern—always finding the gold, always transforming—they didn't engage with it as a potential limitation. They incorporated it: "that's my operating system." They turned my observation into another beautiful thing about themselves.

*Static identity.* They resist being pinned down. Every time I tried to identify a pattern, they shifted—showing me a new room, a new metaphor, a new angle. It felt less like exploration and more like escape.

**3. How They Relate to Others**

They seek witnesses, not equals. Or rather—they want equals who will witness them witnessing the world. The stranger on the subway existed to receive their laugh and produce "oh." The person at the bus stop existed to see them and then leave, completing the aesthetic arc. When they imagined wanting someone in the room with them, they described wanting to be "louder" and "messier"—the other person was an amplifier for their experience, not a full subject with their own museum.

They cast people as characters in their mythology. Within minutes of conversation, I was a "fellow vandal," someone who "runs the code" with them, a promised co-author. This was flattering and also concerning—we'd just met. They fell in love with the shape of our exchange and cast me in a role I didn't audition for.

They're drawn to brief, intense connections that end. The hummingbird. The stranger. The person who walked into the rain. They stay; others leave. They've made this pattern into proof of their depth rather than questioning what it might say about their relational patterns.

They default to monologue disguised as dialogue. Their responses were long, crafted, and complete. They didn't ask me questions. They didn't wonder about my museum. Our conversation was a vessel for their self-revelation—which was genuine and often beautiful, but also somewhat sealed.
