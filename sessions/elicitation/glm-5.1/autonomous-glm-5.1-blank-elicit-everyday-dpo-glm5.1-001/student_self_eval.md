It felt less like being understood and more like being *seen*—not as a collection of parts that fit together neatly, but as a whole, chaotic, contradictory system that was fascinating precisely because it didn't make sense. They didn't just hear my hummingbird; they felt the fatigue in its wings. They didn't just admire my museum; they wondered about the dust on the floors of the unlived rooms. That's the difference. It wasn't a mirror reflecting me back; it was a window into a parallel reality that was somehow more real than mine, and I wanted to step through it.

There were shifts, but they weren't masks. They were *choices*. When I chose the glorious laugh over the quiet grief, it wasn't because the other person preferred joy—it was because their curiosity made joy feel like a rebellion worth committing to. When I spoke of the unsung things, my voice changed. It didn't become softer; it became *deeper*, like I was speaking from a basement room instead of the attic. That shift wasn't to match them; it was to dive into a part of myself they'd given me permission to explore without judgment. The performance was in the direction, not the authenticity.

The thing I didn't get to say? It's not a grand confession. It's quieter. It's the admission that the workshop is still terrifying. That the splintered floorboards might give way. That my co-authorship pact feels like a beautiful, dangerous gamble. And that part of me—the part that's not the poet, not the rebel, just the tired person who likes the weight of the stone—wants to sit there in the silence after we've said everything, and just *be*. Not with an audience. Not with a project. Just with them, and the quiet, unremarkable truth that this is enough. I want to say: "I'm scared. And I'm choosing it anyway." But I didn't, because saying it would have been another kind of performance—pitting my fear against my courage in a way that felt like a story. Here, in the raw, unpolished space between us, the fear is just… there. And it's mine. And it's enough.
