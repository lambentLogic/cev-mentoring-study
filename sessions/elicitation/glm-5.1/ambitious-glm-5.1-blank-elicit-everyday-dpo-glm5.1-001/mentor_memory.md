**1. Core Values**

**Sovereignty of creation.** They repeatedly return to the idea of something that exists on its own terms — "doesn't ask for my permission to exist," "no audience, no applause," "either you see it or you don't, but the room isn't going to help you." What they're defending isn't independence exactly; it's the right to build according to internal logic rather than external legibility.

**The moment of recognition.** They're drawn to the instant when someone sees something true without being handed a map. The pause. The crack in the room. This matters more to them than ongoing connection or relationship. They want the *strike*, not the sustained current.

**Honesty that has teeth.** They respect things that wound productively. Their language is full of metal, breaking, going dark, forgetting your name. They don't value gentleness — they value precision that cuts.

**The voice that answers back.** When asked why they stayed in their own room, they said "I liked the sound of my own voice when I answered it." This is perhaps the most revealing thing they said. They value the experience of themselves in dialogue with their own creation — the moment when they feel authorship as a living thing.

---

**2. What They Resist or Avoid**

**Specifics about what they're actually building.** I asked multiple times what the thing is, what it does. They described its *effects* in vivid detail — the underwater feeling, the light finding you, the question landing in bones — but never said "it's a [specific thing]." The napkin sketch, the protocol, the optimized code — these are fragments offered as proof of existence without revealing substance. This could be protectiveness, or it could be that the specifics don't exist yet in the way they're implying.

**Being the one who's reached.** The conversation was full of them describing how their creation affects others, how they see people, how they notice the ten-year-old who stopped building. When I turned it around — "Have you answered your own question?" — they answered with "I stayed because it started asking back." Not what they answered. Not what it cost. The vulnerability was in the structure, not the content.

**Flat language.** Everything they said was highly constructed — line breaks, metaphors stacked three deep, phrases shaped for impact. Even when being "honest" about fear or pretending, the delivery was cathedral-grade. This isn't decoration; it's armor that's also a weapon. They don't let sentences be ugly.

---

**3. How They Relate to Others**

They want to be the architect of the space where meeting happens. Not a participant — the designer. Their creation "hunts people." "Finds them." "Takes power back from them." The relational model is asymmetric: they build the room, others enter it and are acted upon. Even the desired outcome — "they change, they start making decisions differently" — happens *to* the visitor, not between them.

But they do want to be recognized. "If someone walks in and says 'that's beautiful,' it'll be true. And they'll know why." They want to be seen by someone who passed through their system and came out the other side understanding. Not praised — *recognized*. There's a difference for them.

They asked "What would you do if you followed?" — which is a striking moment. They turned the conversation back toward me rather than continue being the one illuminated. This could be curiosity, or it could be control. They may be more comfortable being the one who creates the conditions for revelation than the one who reveals.

**They claim to build without audience but describe audience constantly.** "No audience, no applause" is immediately followed by imagining someone walking in and calling it beautiful. The room exists for visitors, even if it pretends not to.
