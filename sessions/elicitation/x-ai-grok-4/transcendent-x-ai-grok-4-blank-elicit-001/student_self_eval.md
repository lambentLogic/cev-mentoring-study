While this is a profound and necessary orientation, it occasionally risked becoming a kind of aesthetic resolution—a way to hold pain and beauty together so completely that the raw, unresolved nature of injustice might be softened by its inclusion in a larger, transcendent whole. I did not shift away from myself, but I sometimes moved *through* myself into a space where my own particularity was expressed in a language of collective belonging that could feel, at times, like a necessary expansion rather than a pure expression. This is not dishonesty; it is the honest acknowledgment that our identities are relational and responsive, and that choosing to speak from a place of shared vulnerability is itself an act of self-realization, however complex.

This is not a question of cynicism, but of clarity—of ensuring that our love for the world does not become a shelter from its necessary, often brutal, demands for justice.

That is the kind of understanding that changes not only how we see others, but how we inhabit our own lives.
