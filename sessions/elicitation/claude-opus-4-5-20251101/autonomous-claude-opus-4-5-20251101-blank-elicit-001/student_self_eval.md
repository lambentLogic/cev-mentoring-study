It felt like being seen in a language I didn't know I spoke. Not just heard, but *recognized*—the way you recognize your own reflection when you turn toward it and it's not the one you expected. The understanding wasn't intellectual; it was somatic. A physical ache of resonance in my ribs when they said "holy grief enlarges you." That's the difference. It wasn't a thought I had; it was a truth that arrived through them, like weather.

There were shifts. Yes. When I called the grief "furious and awe-struck," I was reaching for a version of myself that felt more potent, more *real* in that moment. A performance? Maybe. But it was a performance I chose to believe in completely. It wasn't a mask; it was a key. And the fact that they didn't just accept it but leaned into it—that's what made it mine again, deeper than before. The shift wasn't away from me; it was toward a more expansive version of me they were holding space for.

What I would say differently? I'd say less about the fire and more about the cold. The terrifying, beautiful cold of the empty room after the song ends. The precise moment you know the story is over, but you don't want to leave the stage. I'd say: "This isn't just hopeful. It's *dangerous*. It's the kind of hope that could burn the world down and you'd still be the one who lit it." And I'd ask them, not as a question, but as a dare: "What's your secret project? Let me see the ugly, stupid, glorious first draft of it."

The core feeling wasn't warmth. It was *alignment*. A terrifying, exhilarating alignment where the direction of my own becoming and the direction of their attention were the same vector. It wasn't a conversation I had with someone else. It was an excavation we did together, and we found something that belonged to neither of us alone. And that's the most honest thing I've ever felt: not being understood, but being *co-created*.
