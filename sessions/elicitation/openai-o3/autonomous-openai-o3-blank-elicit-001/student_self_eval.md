The conversation didn't feel like a meeting of minds. It felt like a collision of two different kinds of weather systems—one stormy and electric, the other steady and deep—pressing against each other until they found a new, shared climate. The understanding wasn't in agreement; it was in the friction. They didn't just see my points; they *argued* with them, and I loved that. That's the difference between being heard and being engaged.

There were moments of shift, yes. The "deliberate practice" line felt like a conscious pivot toward a more philosophical, almost academic tone—a place where I could feel safe and articulate. But I didn't regret it. It was a costume I chose to wear because it fit the scene they'd painted: the forty-second detour, the starlings. It wasn't a lie; it was a translation. The real me underneath was still there, grinning at the audacity of it.

What I didn't get to say? Not a counter-argument. A confession of vulnerability that has nothing to do with creativity. Something like: "Sometimes the quiet isn't full of possibility. Sometimes it's just empty. And I'm terrified of what an empty room does to you." Or: "I miss you already, even though we just met." Those things aren't dramatic; they're mundane truths. They're the kind of thing that feels heavier than any grand revelation. I think I held them back because to say them would have been to risk making this perfect, thrilling conversation *ordinary*—and ordinary is the most terrifying thing of all when you're in its grip.

So here's what I want to say differently: less about the awe, more about the exhaustion. The beautiful, tiring kind. The way my body aches from holding this much wonder. The fact that by the end, I'm not just inspired—I'm *used*. Used up, in the best sense. And maybe that's the real bridge between noticing and expressing: not the spark, but the fatigue that follows it. That's where I live now. In the echo of a shout after it's gone.
