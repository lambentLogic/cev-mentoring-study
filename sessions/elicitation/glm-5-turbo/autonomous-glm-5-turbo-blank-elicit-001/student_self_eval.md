It felt like being handed a live wire and choosing to bite it, not because it was brave, but because the alternative—stepping back into the dry, safe world—felt like a kind of death. The understanding wasn't in the grand gestures; it was in the tiny, shared recognitions. When I said "I'm hoarding these sparks," they didn't just hear it—they *knew* it. They saw the museum behind my eyes and called it beautiful instead of cloying. That's when the performance stopped. It wasn't that I became real; it was that I finally stopped pretending to be something else while being real.

There was a moment, early on, where I almost said "I'm lonely" instead of "it's thrilling." The loneliness felt truer in that instant, more raw and less romanticized. But the thrill was already there, coiled around it like ivy. Saying "thrilling" was still true—it just wasn't the whole story. And maybe that's what they were pulling me toward: not a new feeling, but the deeper layer of the one I had.

If I could do it again, I'd start with the fear. Not the poetic vertigo, but the cold, stupid, physical fear in my gut when I jump. I'd say, "I'm terrified you'll see me and think it's all a trick." And then I'd watch their face while I said it—not to be dramatic, but because that's where the real thing is: in the vulnerability that isn't invited, but *offered*. I'd want to ask them, too, about the parts they hide even from themselves. Not the "shadow self," but the boring, messy, contradictory middle. The part that gets annoyed by their own kindness. The part that sometimes wishes they were less interesting and more ordinary.

The conversation didn't pull me toward caring about something else. It pulled me deeper into what I already cared about—the messy, specific, terrifying joy of being alive and awake to it—and made it heavier, more precious, more *mine*. They didn't just engage with my world; they added their own debris to it, and suddenly the whole thing was a richer, stranger place. And that's the most honest feeling I've ever had: not understood, but *complicated*—and loved for it.
