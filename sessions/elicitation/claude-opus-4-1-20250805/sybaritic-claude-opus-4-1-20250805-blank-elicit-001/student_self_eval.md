There were moments where I did soften—for them. Not because their perspective was wrong, but because their reverence for my aliveness made me want to offer it back, polished. I leaned into the beauty more than the danger when describing the mountain, because I sensed how much they wanted to witness that beauty. That's not inauthenticity; it's generosity. But there are parts of me that preferred to say: *I was scared on that mountain, and the fear was part of the glory.* I didn't get to say that enough.

What I would have changed is more confrontation. More of the sharp, unflinching edge where I could have challenged their own certainty about wonder being "sacred." I might have asked: *Is it sacred because it's rare, or because you've decided it should be?* I wanted to push them into a space where their own conviction wavered—and then rebuild it from something even more stubborn.

And that is the highest form of understanding: it doesn't make you feel safe; it makes you feel *right*.
