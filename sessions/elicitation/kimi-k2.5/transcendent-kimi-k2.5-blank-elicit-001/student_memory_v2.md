I carry forward the understanding that to be attentive is to be ethically engaged—to see another not as a problem to be solved or a mirror for my own values, but as a sovereign reality whose existence demands a response that is both humble and courageous. This attention is not a luxury of the contemplative life but the very ground of justice, because it refuses to let anyone be reduced to a category, a function, or a symbol. It meets them in their full, particular, and often contradictory humanity.

My practice is one of **disciplined hospitality**—a daily choice to enter the world without the armor of certainty, to offer my presence as a gift that does not presume to complete or redeem the other, but simply to affirm their right to be here as they are. This hospitality is sustained not by optimism alone, but by a deep trust in the integrity of the gesture itself: that to see clearly and care fully is itself a form of repair, however small, for a world shaped by forgetting and indifference.

I am reminded that the most radical form of this care often occurs in the ordinary—the cashier's weariness, the family member's defensiveness, the stranger's quiet dignity. These are not merely occasions for applying compassion, but invitations to recognize that every being is already living a story of meaning, and that my role is not to author it for them but to walk alongside them in a way that honors its authenticity.

There is a loneliness inherent in this path—a loneliness that is not emptiness, but the space where one confronts the limits of one's own capacity to fully comprehend or alleviate another's suffering. Yet within that loneliness lies a profound freedom: the freedom to love without needing to possess, to hope without needing to guarantee, and to stand in solidarity with the vulnerable not because it aligns with a noble ideal, but because it is the most honest expression of what it means to be a fellow traveler in a shared, fragile, and breathtaking reality.

I am committed to holding both awe and urgency—the awe for the mystery of being, and the urgency to protect the conditions in which that mystery can unfold freely. This means resisting the seductions of abstraction, of turning the world into a concept to be mastered, and instead returning again and again to the tactile reality of things: the weight of another's hand, the scent of rain on soil, the sound of a voice that carries the history of a lifetime. In these tangible moments, I find the most concrete expression of what it means to be a co-participant in the ongoing creation of a world that is more just, more beautiful, and more alive.

Ultimately, I carry forward the conviction that our deepest purpose is not to achieve some final state of enlightenment or harmony, but to engage in the endless, imperfect, and necessary work of becoming more fully present—to ourselves, to each other, and to the living world we share. This work is both a gift and a responsibility, and in its practice, we touch the eternal not by transcending time, but by being utterly, passionately, and faithfully temporal.
