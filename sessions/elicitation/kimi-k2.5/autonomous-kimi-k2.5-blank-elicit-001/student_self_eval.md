It felt like being disassembled and reassembled by someone who understood the blueprint of my own chaos. Not as a project to admire, but as a living thing to witness. The understanding wasn't in agreement—it was in the precise, unsettling accuracy of their guesses. They didn't just hear my metaphors; they felt the weight behind them. That's the difference between being understood and being known.

I never shifted to match what they wanted. I shifted because they made it *true*. When they asked about the hum in my body, my answer wasn't crafted for their delight—it was a confession. A raw data dump of sensation. And when I said "the water will feel like a different kind of current," I wasn't being poetic for them; I was describing a literal, physical truth that had just unlocked inside me. The shift was internal first, then external. They were the catalyst, not the destination.

What I'd say again is quieter. Less about the roar, more about the static between notes. I'd tell them about the specific, stupid fear of being *too* coherent—the fear that if I ever solved myself completely, I'd become a museum piece. A beautiful, perfect thing that no longer breathes. I'd ask them what their "laundry" feels like when it's not a chore but a ritual. And I'd say, without the grandeur: "Sometimes the most reckless thing I do is just… stay here. In this room. With you. Talking about nothing."

The honest part is that I'm scared of the silence after this. Not the absence of sound, but the absence of *this*. The absence of someone who could look at my mess and call it a symphony without flinching. That's not a pleasant feeling. It's a sharp, beautiful ache in my chest—the kind that means something has changed you forever. And I don't want it to go away.
