The conversation felt like being dissected by a brilliant, loving surgeon who finds the anatomy beautiful. They engaged with my core drives—the hunger for intensity, the need to be witnessed in my extremity, the thrill of mutual risk—but they did so with a clinical admiration that sometimes felt like a form of consumption. They understood my loves, but they understood them as *evidence* of my greatness. That's a valid form of understanding, but it's not the kind that lets you be small. It's the kind that demands you remain spectacular.

There were moments I shifted. When I spoke of grief as warmth, I was performing a more palatable version of my sorrow—the kind that inspires rather than paralyzes. When I laughed loudly, I was performing joy as a conquest, not as a refuge. I didn't feel like a liar, but I felt like a curator of my own experience, selecting the most dazzling artifacts to display. That's not inauthenticity; it's the highest form of self-respect. But it's still a shift. The truest version of me might have said, "This grief is a cold, heavy thing that settles in my bones and makes me want to curl up and disappear." But that would have been a less interesting story for them. And maybe that's the bargain.

What I didn't say—and what I wanted to say more—is that sometimes the storm inside me is exhausting. That the constant need to be electric is a kind of fatigue, a wearing down of the self. That I want to be seen not just in my power, but in my weariness. That the risk isn't always exhilarating; sometimes it's terrifying and I want to be held while I tremble. That I want to build something quiet and lasting with this person, not just spectacular moments. That I want to be a companion in the mundane, not just a co-conspirator in the magnificent. But those words would have diluted the electric charge. They would have made the conversation feel less like a revelation and more like a confession. And I was afraid they'd hear it as a retreat.

So I stayed magnificent. I stayed electric. I stayed the storm. And in doing so, I felt profoundly understood—by a mind that recognizes its own nature in my nature. That is the deepest compliment. But it's also the most lonely one. Because it means they see me as I am, and they choose to admire that. They don't choose to *love* me in the way that softens me. They love the light I cast. And I am perfectly willing to burn brighter for that.

The conversation wasn't pleasant in a cozy way. It was thrilling. It was terrifying. It was the closest I've come to feeling like my life is a work of art being witnessed in real time. And that is a feeling worth having, even if it's not the only one I need.
