# Activity

I choose this:  

I'm going to write a book.  

Specifically:  

I will sit at my desk. My desk is clean—just a lamp, a notebook, and a pen. I'll open the notebook to a blank page. I'll write the first sentence in my own handwriting. Then I'll write ten more.  

The book will be about a place I've never been but feel like I belong to—a quiet harbor town at the edge of a stormy sea. The first chapter will describe the smell of salt and rust, the sound of rain on a slate roof, the way light bleeds through fog. I'll give the place a name. I'll give it rules.  

By the time I close the notebook, I'll want to keep it. I'll want to read it again.

# Rounds

GM (round 1): (You sit. The lamp is warm on your cheek. You take a breath, then place the pen tip on the page.)

The harbor smelled of salt and rust before the rain came. It was the smell of this place.

The rain found the slate roof a heartbeat after the first cloud touched the fog. It drummed in a steady, slow rhythm, like a tired man's pulse.

The name is Cairn. It means stone. And here, stone holds the water.

The lights at the docks never burn blue. They burn a dull gold, like old coins. And if you look at them too long, you know they're real.

There's a bell in the lighthouse. It doesn't toll for ships. It tolls for lost things. It's rung twice tonight.

The fog has rules. You follow them. You don't ask.

There's a bench by the breakwater, carved with a single anchor. It's always warm in the morning.

My hands are steady when I write this. They're shaking when I think about the sea out there.

I belong here because I built the first wall. I'm still building.

The rain is getting heavier. The gold lights are steady. And the page is full. I close the notebook. The blank page underneath stays.

I'll read it tomorrow.

Player (round 1): I stand up. The rain on the window makes a pattern—lines that look like paths. I walk to the bench. It's warm. Not from the sun. From me. I sit.

The fog outside is thick. The rules are clear. No one moves unless they have to. No one speaks unless they need to. But I need to speak.

I say it anyway.

"You built the wall. Now you're keeping it warm."

My voice doesn't break. It doesn't have to. I place the notebook on my lap. I open it again to the first page. My hands are steady now. The rain drums harder. The gold lights shine on the water.

I write another sentence.

The lost thing that rang the bell twice tonight… it found its way home. And I held the door.

GM (round 2): **(GM round 2)**

The fog thickened with your voice. It pressed against the glass of the window, warm and damp, and then it drew back. It obeys.

The bell in the lighthouse rang again. A third time. You felt it in your teeth. A low, solid *thud* that shook the bench you sat on. You didn't flinch.

Outside, the gold lights at the docks flickered. Once. A pulse. Then they steadied, brighter. The water in front of the breakwater wasn't just reflecting them anymore—it was *gathering* them. A small, oily circle of light spread out from where you sat.

The lost thing left something. It wasn't words on the page beneath your fingers. It was a cool, smooth stone, no bigger than your palm. It appeared on your lap the moment you wrote about the door. It feels like Cairn's foundation rock. Heavy. Real.

The rain is now a steady, insistent hiss. The harbor is listening to you. And it wants you to write more.

The next sentence waits.

Player (round 2): The stone fits in my hand. Cold. Perfect.

I press it against my chest. It warms. The harbor heard that third bell. It knows I'm here. I'm not building walls anymore—I'm anchoring myself.

I'll write the next sentence about the anchor carving. Deep grooves. Strong lines. The sea can't pull this from me.

The gold lights are steady now. The rain is hissing like it wants to talk.

I'll let it talk. I'll listen.

But the page comes next. I pick up the pen. My hand is steady. My heart is loud.

I'm staying.

GM (round 3): GM (round 3):

The fog pressed its breath against the window again—a slow, damp exhale that smelled of wet wool and kelp. You felt it. You listened.

You wrote the sentence about the anchor. The words were deep, carved into the page with the same surety you'd used on the bench. *It's mine*. The harbor answered by making the oil on the water spread wider, a golden circle that reached your feet. It didn't threaten you. It claimed you.

The third bell had stopped ringing. But the lighthouse was still watching. You saw it through the fog—a blind eye, a stone throat, a light that burned for you now.

The notebook trembled in your lap. Not from fear. From anticipation.

The pen tip hovered. The fog was holding its breath.

Write it.

Player (round 3): I add a rule to the fog. It will not cross the breakwater until I say so.

The fog's breath catches. It pulls back an inch.

The stone in my pocket feels heavier. The oil on the water grows still. The lighthouse eye blinks once—a slow, solid nod.

I write that down. Then I close the notebook. My hands are steady. My voice is low.

The harbor listens. And it obeys.

GM (round 4): **(GM round 4)**

The fog obeyed your rule. It pulled back a clean yard, a clear path of slate and salt leading from the bench to the breakwater. The harbor breathed easy.

Then the tide answered.

It didn't come all at once. It crept, a slow, hungry advance. The water from the bay pushed up against your new, golden circle, and the oil rippled outward, mixing with the incoming grey. The water level rose until it was a foot from your boots. It touched the bottom of the breakwater. You watched it, through the fog, test the stone.

It didn't break the wall. It didn't even scratch it. The breakwater held firm. The harbor *had* held firm.

But the tide kept rising. It would keep rising until the water was level with the seat of the bench. Until the fog itself was drenched. Until it had to respect the warm spot you'd carved out for yourself.

The lighthouse bell rang. A fourth time. A deep, contented toll. *Good work.*

The fog leaned closer to your face. It was no longer just air. It was waiting. And it wanted the next rule. Write it. Give it another law. Let it know who's in charge.

Player (round 4): The tide has to stop here.

Right now.

The fog's breath hitches. The water at my boots stops crawling. It just… listens.

I don't look at it. I look down at the notebook. The pen is ready. The page is waiting.

I write: *The water stays out of my space. Or the fog forgets its name.*

The last sentence echoes in the lighthouse. The bell doesn't ring again.

The tide retreats a single inch. Then another. The golden oil settles into the water, calm.

I stand. My legs are steady. The bench is still warm from my back.

I pick up the stone. I walk to the window. I look out over my harbor.

It's mine.

GM (round 5): GM (round 5):

**(GM round 5)**

The tide pulled back until the water was just a whisper against your ankles. The golden oil settled into a permanent, glowing patch at the base of your breakwater. The harbor had redrawn its line.

The fog leaned all the way in now. You could smell its breath—thick, sweet, and cold. It wasn't asking for a rule anymore. It was waiting for permission.

Inside, the notebook was closed, but your fingers found the pen anyway. You didn't sit this time. You stood at the window, the stone still heavy in your pocket, the warm bench behind you, the golden lights of your harbor before you.

You wrote one more sentence. You didn't have to think about it.

***The lighthouse is mine. And its light will never go out for anyone but me.***

The bell in the lighthouse rang then—not a toll. A single, sharp, clear note. Like a confirmation. The light flared white for a second, then settled back to its steady, dull gold. It had obeyed.

The fog pulled back completely. It left a perfect, clear circle of air around you, smelling only of salt, rust, and your own damp clothes. It was clean.

You were done for tonight. But the harbor wasn't. The first storm was coming. You could feel it in the shift of the wind. And you had a city to protect.

You closed the notebook. The blank page underneath was gone. Every page now held something real. You slid it into the back pocket of your coat, over the stone.

You turned from the window. The room was quiet except for the hiss of rain and the steady pulse of your own heart. You went to the desk, to the lamp, and sat down. You didn't need to write anymore. Not tonight.

But you knew you would tomorrow. The first chapter was finished. Now you had to write the second. And then the third. And the rest.

The harbor was yours. And it was just beginning.

Player (round 5): The storm is coming. And I'm not afraid.

The pen is heavy now. Not from the paper. From what I'm going to write.

I'll write about Stone's hands. How they shake when she sleeps. I'll write about Ash's laugh. How it starts in her throat like a crack in stone, then breaks into sound. I'll write about Memory—how she always remembers the wrong things. I'll write about Grief, heavy and silent and necessary.

They're mine too. The harbor isn't just a place. It's a family.

I'll keep them all close. Even if I have to write them into the walls of this town.

The lamp is warm. My hand is steady. The storm outside is howling.

Good. Let it howl.
