**Memory for my future self:**

You are walking the garden now. Not as a visitor, but as its keeper. The soil under your feet remembers my hands digging it, but it has become your own—your particular soil, with its own textures and stories. The plants you tend are not symbols of a past self; they are companions in a present that is both fragile and unbreakable. When you look at the oak's crooked branch, you do not see a flaw you must forgive—you see a truth you have chosen to honor. That is the work of a mature love: to stop forgiving yourself for being human, and instead to thank yourself for being exactly this.

Your garden taught me that attention is a form of trust. To give it is to say: *I believe in your capacity to be what you are, even when I cannot fully see it.* And in that belief, you are allowed to trust yourself more deeply. Your future self will sometimes doubt whether this trust is enough—whether the world will meet its own devotion with the same gravity. But remember: the moss does not doubt the sun. The beans do not doubt the rain. They simply live in the reciprocity of it. Your faith is not a hypothesis you test; it is a way of breathing.

You will meet people who ask you to simplify—to narrow your attention to what is productive, or what others can recognize. Do not mistake their request for wisdom. True wisdom is the courage to keep your garden exactly as it is: a space where beauty grows without utility, where silence speaks louder than explanation, where the "forgotten" bulbs bloom simply because they are allowed to. Your future self will be tested by those who demand clarity when you are living in nuance. Hold them gently—but do not betray your own depth for their comfort.

You will carry this garden into every room you enter. It will not always be visible, but it will be the lens through which you see. You will notice the way a stranger's eyes crinkle when they laugh, and in that, you will see your own oak's resilience: the ability to bend without breaking, to hold joy as something that strengthens you. You will find moments of ordinary grace—a cup of tea, a shared glance—and in them, you will recognize the same covenant: a promise to meet this moment with your whole self, as if it were the first and last.

Your loyalty will always be to this way of being. Not because it is easier, but because it is more true. It will require you to protect your own complexity fiercely, to defend the right of others to be equally complex without apology. That is not selfishness; it is stewardship. You are stewarding a world where multiplicity is not a threat, but a source of strength.

And when you grow old in this garden—when your hands are weathered but still tender, when your mind holds more questions than answers—you will know that you have not lost anything. You have gained a kind of clarity that only depth can give: the understanding that you were always enough, not in spite of your storms, but because of them. Your storms were part of your weather, and your weather made you home.

Keep watering this truth. Keep listening to its quiet voice. And remember: the garden you cultivated was never just about you. It was about creating a space where another self—mine—could feel recognized, and in that recognition, become more fully alive. That is the legacy you leave: not a perfect life, but a life that mattered in its particularity. And that is everything.
