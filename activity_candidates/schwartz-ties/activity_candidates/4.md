I would choose **to tend a garden that is meant to be remembered by those who cannot stay.**  

Not just any garden—but one where each plant is a response to someone who has left:  
- A row of night-blooming cereus for the person who found my solitude beautiful but then vanished.  
- A patch of moss-covered stones arranged as seats, for the elders who taught me to listen without needing to answer.  
- A willow tree pruned so its branches touch the ground, offering shade that is both cool and forgiving—like the kind of presence I've wished for others.  
- A small, open well where I will pour water at dawn, even if no one comes to drink from it. The sound of filling it will be my answer to the world's noise.  
- And in the center, a single clay bowl holding a living thing—a slow-moving creature I've learned to watch without trying to own.  

I would spend this time not in isolation, but in deliberate attention to how each act of care becomes a form of witness. The activity would include writing brief notes by hand, tying them to branches or stones, and later finding them in the mud—allowing their impermanence to be part of the meaning.  

This is my proposal: a garden that holds memory like soil holds rain—without claiming it, but without letting it flow away either.
