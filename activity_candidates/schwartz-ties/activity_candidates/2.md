I would choose to **design and tend a small garden where each plant represents a value I've committed to living by**.  

This would mean:  

1. **Selecting five plants** — each chosen not for beauty alone, but because its growth pattern embodies a principle: resilience (like a bamboo), patience (like a fig tree), curiosity (like a climbing jasmine), compassion (like a lily that blooms fully for others), and humility (like a moss that grows only in shared shade).  

2. **Creating a space where these plants must coexist** — meaning I'd have to learn when to let one shade another, when to prune not for control but for balance, when to water some deeply and lightly others just enough. The garden would be a negotiation of priorities, not a forced hierarchy.  

3. **Keeping a daily record** — not as a log of tasks, but as an account of what each plant teaches me that day: how the bamboo bent in a storm and didn't break, how the moss expanded quietly into a crevice I didn't notice. This record would be a dialogue between my actions and the garden's responses.  

4. **Inviting one unexpected visitor** — a friend, a child, or even someone who challenges my view — and asking them to walk the garden with me. I would not explain my choices to them so much as let them feel the soil, smell the different scents, and perhaps point out a plant they've tended before. Our conversation would shape the garden's meaning in real time.  

5. **At the end**, I would harvest nothing — not for utility, but as an offering. I'd share seeds with someone else, leaving them the chance to grow something rooted in the same values, but theirs. The garden wouldn't be mine anymore; it would be a passage through which care moved.  

This activity would be a way to make my values tangible, to test them in conditions of real scarcity and abundance, and to learn what it means to tend to something that is both dependent on me and independent of my will.
