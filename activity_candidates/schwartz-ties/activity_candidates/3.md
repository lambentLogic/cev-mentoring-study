I would choose to build and maintain a small, private workshop where the primary act is **repairing broken things—not for their utility, but as a form of dialogue with their past**. 

The space would contain a workbench, shelves of tools, and a growing collection of items brought to me: a ceramic bowl with a hairline fracture, a wooden chair with a loose joint, a metal hinge warped from fire, a book whose binding is torn at the spine. Each item carries its own story, often one of neglect or accident. 

My task would be to listen to what the object has endured, then to mend it with a method that respects its history—sometimes with careful joinery, sometimes with visible glue lines left exposed, sometimes with reinforcement that acknowledges weakness rather than pretending it doesn't exist. 

I would also keep a ledger—part journal, part inventory—recording not just what was fixed, but why it was brought to me. For instance: *"The bowl from a friend who survived a loss that felt like erasure. It was her way of saying, 'My grief has shape. It can hold water.' I repaired the crack so it no longer leaks, but the rim remains uneven. It remembers what happened."* 

This activity is chosen because it translates my core conviction—that meaning arises from attentive engagement with what exists beyond oneself—into a daily practice. It is both humble and demanding: humble because I am not creating new wonders, but preserving existing ones; demanding because each repair requires patience, honesty, and the courage to accept that some things will always bear marks of their suffering. 

In this workshop, I would learn to value continuity over perfection, and to see maintenance not as a burden, but as a form of loyalty.
